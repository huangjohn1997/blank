What this guide is for

Every model launch produces two documents. One is the headline: bigger numbers, same price. The other is the boring one — the migration note, the behavior-change list, the paragraph in the system card where an evaluation didn't go the way anyone expected. The second document is the one that changes how you actually work.

This guide is an attempt at the second document for Claude Opus 5. It assumes you already know that it's good at coding. It concentrates on the things that will surprise you in week one: why your prompts now produce longer answers, why your carefully-tuned "verify your work" instruction is now making output worse, why lowering effort doesn't shorten replies, why a code-review prompt that asks for restraint now returns fewer real bugs, and what the card admits about the model's own uncertainty.

Chapter One

What kind of release this is

Opus 5 is unusual in the family in one specific way: it is a step-change model shipped at a maintenance-release price. Anthropic's own documentation calls it "a step-change improvement rather than an incremental one," and then prices it identically to Opus 4.8 — $5 per million input tokens, $25 per million output. The interesting consequence is that the decision to migrate is not economic. It is behavioral. You are not trading money for capability; you are trading a set of habits.

1.1The positioning nobody states plainly

The family now has two frontiers, not one. Fable 5 is described as Anthropic's most capable widely released model and costs $10/$50. Opus 5 sits at half that price and, on Anthropic's framing, comes close to Fable's intelligence. Third-party measurement broadly agrees: on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, Opus 5 at max effort reportedly scores 61 against Fable 5's 60.1

Read the specs sidewaysOpus 5's knowledge cutoff (May 2026) is four months later than Fable 5's (Jan 2026). For anything touching recent libraries, APIs, or events, the cheaper model is the better-informed one.

That inversion is the first non-obvious fact of this release: the flagship is not strictly ahead on every axis. Opus 5 is newer, cheaper, faster, and knows more recent things. Fable 5 remains the pick for a specific shape of work — long-horizon autonomy measured in hours or days — which Chapter 8 unpacks.

1.2Where the gains actually landed

The documented improvement areas over Opus 4.8, in the order they'll matter to you:

Agentic coding
Finishes instead of stubbing
Multi-file features, large refactors, end-to-end work — explicitly "without leaving stubs or placeholders." Performs best given the whole spec up front and then left alone.
Review
High precision and recall
Finds real bugs at a high rate per pass, and the extra findings are mostly real rather than noise. Accuracy holds at lower effort — which enables a cheap pass now, thorough pass later.
Economy

The bottom of the effort ladder now beats the same settings on earlier Opus models. This is the single biggest cost lever in the release and the most under-used.
Long context

Instruction-following, tool calling, and reasoning stay consistent throughout the window — not just retrieval accuracy, which is what most long-context claims mean.
Vision

Strong on charts, documents, diagrams, UI replication — and strongest when given tools to crop, analyze, and visually verify. Tool access is a more cost-effective lever than raising effort.
Orchestration

Office work

Scaling

Test-time compute scaling improved: additional effort up to max actually buys results, which was less reliably true on earlier models.
Why this matters more than the benchmark

"Finds real bugs with few false positives" and "effort converts to quality" are statements about the shape of your workflow, not about a score. The first means a review pass is now worth reading in full. The second means an effort sweep on your own evals is a real optimization with real headroom — not a rounding error.

Chapter Two

Two breaking changes, and the second one is sharp

Most Claude migrations are a string swap. This one is a string swap plus two things that will throw errors or bills at you if you skip the note. Both concern thinking, and both are easy to miss precisely because they only bite configurations that were previously fine.

2.1Thinking is on by default

On Opus 4.8, a request with no thinking field ran without thinking. On Opus 5, the identical request runs with adaptive thinking: the model decides when and how much to think, per turn. The wire format didn't change — thinking: {"type":"adaptive"} remains valid and is now simply equivalent to the default.

The bill, not the errorThis one usually doesn't 400. It quietly changes your token accounting, because max_tokens is a hard ceiling on thinking plus response text combined.
⚠ The silent truncation

If a workload ran without thinking on 4.8 and had max_tokens tuned tightly to its expected answer length, that budget is now shared with thinking. The response can hit the ceiling and truncate with stop_reason: "max_tokens" — output that looks like the model "stopped mid-sentence" rather than like a config error. Revisit max_tokens on every workload that previously ran thinking-free.

2.2You can no longer disable thinking at the top of the ladder

thinking: {"type":"disabled"} still works — but only at effort high or below. Combine it with xhigh or max and you get a 400 error. Opus 4.8 accepted that combination happily, which is exactly why it's worth auditing: the requests that break are the ones that used to be legal.

Rejected on Opus 5 · accepted on Opus 4.8
client.messages.create(
    model="claude-opus-5",
    max_tokens=16000,
    thinking={"type": "disabled"},
    output_config={"effort": "xhigh"},   # ← 400 error
    messages=[...],
)

Two ways out. Either keep the effort level and drop the thinking field (thinking is on by default anyway), or keep thinking disabled and step effort down to high or lower. The validation is per request, not per conversation — a mid-session escalation to xhigh is rejected even if every earlier turn in the same conversation was accepted.

◆ Quirk · The two artifacts of a thinking-free Opus 5

Disabling thinking is legal but it costs you output hygiene. Two artifacts occasionally surface in visible text:

  • Tool calls rendered as text. The model writes what should have been a structured tool_use block into its user-facing prose instead. The turn completes normally and the call never runs. Worse, in an agentic loop the leaked text stays in conversation history, so later turns are contaminated too. Most common on tool-heavy workloads like search.
  • Internal XML tags in the response. <thinking> and similar tags can leak into visible output.

The documented mitigation for the first is counterintuitive — give it permission to talk, so it stops smuggling the call into prose: "You may say a brief sentence before using a tool."

And for the second, an instruction telling the model not to think or not to reason increases tag leakage. Remove those. Use the general form — "Do not include internal or system XML tags in your response." — because instructions that name thinking tags specifically work less well than the general one.

✓ The actual recommendation

Don't disable thinking to save money. Keep it on and use lower effort as the cost control. That is Anthropic's stated primary mitigation for both artifacts, and on this model the low end of the ladder is genuinely good.

Chapter Three

The effort ladder, re-read

Effort is the most misunderstood parameter in the current API, and Opus 5 adds a twist that breaks the most common mental model of it. If you take one operational idea from this guide, take this one: effort governs how much the model works, not how much it says.

3.1What effort actually touches

Effort is a behavioral signal applied to all tokens in the response — text, thinking, and tool calls alike. That last one is the part people miss. Lower effort doesn't just shorten reasoning; it changes agentic strategy:

At lower effort
Fewer, fatter tool calls
Operations get combined, total call count drops, the model proceeds straight to action without preamble, and confirmations are terse.
At higher effort
More exploration, more narration
More tool calls, an explained plan before acting, detailed change summaries, and more comprehensive code comments.

So effort is not a "quality dial." It is a thoroughness of process dial, and process is where agentic cost lives. Note too that effort is a signal, not a budget: at low effort the model will still think on a genuinely hard problem — just less than it would have.

⚠ The counterintuitive one

On Opus 5, lowering effort does not reliably shorten the visible response. It reduces thinking volume. If you have been using effort as a verbosity control — a habit that half-worked on earlier models — it will now fail in the most annoying way possible: you'll pay less, wait less, and still get a wall of text. Length is a prompting problem on this model. Chapter 4 has the wording.

✓ Do this once, benefit for months

Re-run an effort sweep on your own evals. Anthropic says this outright: don't carry over an effort setting tuned for an earlier model. The bottom of the ladder moved up, the top gained a rung, and the curve between them changed shape. A setting that was optimal on 4.8 is now, at best, a coincidence.

3.3Three practical traps

Trap
max_tokens too small at xhigh/max
At the top two levels the model needs room to think and act across tool calls and subagents. Start at 64k max_tokens and tune. A tight ceiling at max effort is a truncation waiting to happen.
Trap
Varying effort mid-conversation
Effort shapes the rendered prompt, so changing it between requests invalidates your cached prefix. Vary effort across workloads, not within a cached session. Pick a level at the start and hold it.
Trap
Passing adaptive as an effort value
adaptive is a thinking mode, not an effort level. The two parameters are adjacent and easy to conflate; they are not interchangeable.
◆ Quirk · "ultracode" is not an effort level

If you use Claude Code, ultracode appears in the effort menu — but the API accepts no such value. Ultracode is xhigh effort plus standing permission for Claude Code to launch multi-agent workflows, granted through mid-conversation system messages. Useful to know when you try to reproduce Claude Code's behavior through the API and can't find the missing setting: the missing piece is a permission, not a compute tier.

3.4Task budgets: the other dial, and its trap

Effort tunes depth per step. Task budgets (beta, task-budgets-2026-03-13) tune total breadth across an entire agentic loop. You hand the model a token allowance; it sees a server-injected countdown and paces itself, finishing gracefully rather than getting cut off mid-action.

◆ Quirk · A budget that's too small reads as a refusal

Give the model a budget clearly insufficient for the work — say 20,000 tokens for a multi-hour coding task — and it may decline to attempt the task at all, aggressively scope it down, or stop early with a partial result rather than start work it can't finish. This presents as a mysterious refusal or premature stop. The documented advice is blunt: if you see unexpected refusals after setting a budget, raise the budget before debugging anything else.

Three more things about budgets that are easy to get wrong:

  • The countdown is model-only. There is no remaining-budget field in the API response and no SDK accessor. You cannot read it; you can only set it.
  • It counts what the model sees, not what you send. Resent history isn't re-counted. If you resend full history and decrement remaining yourself, the model sees an under-reported budget and wraps up far too early. Set it generously once and let the server track it.
  • It's advisory. The model may exceed it if interrupting an in-flight action would be more disruptive than finishing. max_tokens remains the only hard ceiling. Minimum accepted budget is 20,000 tokens; below that, a 400.

Not available on Claude Code or Cowork surfaces — Messages API only, and notably not supported on Sonnet 5.

Chapter Four

Seven drifts you'll notice without changing a line of code

This is the chapter that earns the guide. Anthropic documents a set of behavioral differences that appear on identical prompts — no config change required. Several of them mean that instructions which improved output on Opus 4.8 now degrade it on Opus 5. Prompt libraries carried forward unexamined are the main way teams get worse results from a better model.

4.1Drift one: default responses run longer

Visible, user-facing answers are simply longer than on prior Opus models. And per Chapter 3, effort won't fix it. Prompt for it:

Conciseness · for a user-facing multi-turn product
Keep responses focused, brief, and concise. Keep disclaimers and caveats short, and spend most of the response on the main answer. When asked to explain something, give a high-level summary unless an in-depth explanation is specifically requested.

In a long system prompt, pair that with a short reminder near the end of the prompt — position matters when the instruction has to compete with thousands of tokens of context.

Recency as a leverA two-line <tone_preference> block at the tail of a long prompt is doing something structural, not stylistic: it re-asserts the constraint at the point closest to generation.
Tail reminder
<tone_preference>
Keep outputs reasonably concise.
</tone_preference>

4.2Drift two: written deliverables run longer too

Separate phenomenon, separate fix. Files the model writes to disk — reports, Markdown docs, summaries — are often longer than on prior models. Conversational brevity instructions don't govern them.

Deliverable length calibration
Match the length of written documents to what the task needs: cover the substance, but do not pad with filler sections, redundant summaries, or boilerplate.

4.3Drift three: it narrates more during agentic work

Opus 5 announces what it's about to do, and per-message output in agentic sessions is longer. The documented fix is not "be quiet" — it's describe the cadence and shape you want:

Narration cadence · tuning down
Before your first tool call, say in one sentence what you're about to do. While working, give a brief update only when you find something important or change direction. When you finish, lead with the outcome: your first sentence should answer "what happened" or "what did you find," with supporting detail after it for readers who want it.
A general principle worth internalizing

Anthropic notes that positive examples of the communication style you want are more effective than instructions about what not to do. That holds in both directions — the same lever turns narration up. Describe the target, don't prohibit the deviation.

4.4Drift four: it verifies itself — so delete your verification instructions

⚠ The instruction that is now actively harmful

Opus 5 verifies its own work without being told to. If your prompt says "include a final verification step for any non-trivial task" or "use a subagent to verify," remove it. Those instructions compound with the model's native behavior and cause over-verification: wasted tokens, wasted wall-clock, no gain in quality. The same applies to legacy harness scaffolding that bolts on a separate verification stage.

The same logic extends to self-correction prompts. "Double-check your answer" and "re-verify before responding" now duplicate something the model already does well, adding cost without improving results.

This is worth sitting with, because it inverts several years of accumulated prompt-engineering folk wisdom. The prompts most likely to hurt you on Opus 5 are the ones you added because they used to help.

4.5Drift five: it expands scope

Opus 5 can add steps that weren't requested, or apply its own judgment about what the task should be. Reasonable in an ambiguous brief; unwelcome on a narrow one. Constrain explicitly:

Scope constraint · for narrow tasks
Deliver what was asked, at the scope intended. Make routine judgment calls yourself, and check in only when different readings of the request would lead to materially different work. If the request seems mistaken or a better approach exists, say so in a sentence and continue with the task as asked rather than quietly narrowing, widening, or transforming it. Finish the whole task, and stop short of actions that are clearly beyond what was asked.

4.6Drift six: it delegates to subagents more readily

Delegation is a genuine strength here — writer–verifier patterns work, and agents rarely overwrite each other. But eagerness has a cost curve: delegation multiplies both spend and latency when applied to small tasks. Give explicit criteria, or cap spawn counts deterministically in your harness.

Note the overlap"Do not use subagents to verify your own work" appears in both the delegation and the over-verification guidance. It's the single most expensive redundant pattern on this model.
Delegation policy · cost-sensitive workloads
Delegate to a subagent only for large tasks that are genuinely independent and parallelizable, such as a wide multi-file investigation. Do not delegate work you can finish yourself in a handful of tool calls, and do not use subagents to verify or double-check your own work. If one subagent can complete the task, use one rather than several, and keep spawn counts low.

4.7Drift seven: it narrates its own corrections more

The model catches its own mistakes well — and then tells you about it, more than prior models did. In a user-facing product that reads as hedging, or as instability. Bound it to corrections that matter:

Correction discipline
Only correct an earlier statement when the error would change the user's code, conclusions, or decisions. State corrections plainly and briefly, then continue the task. For slips that change nothing for the user, make the fix and move on without noting it.

4.8The best single nugget in the docs

◆ Quirk · Asking a good reviewer for restraint makes it a worse reviewer

Opus 5 reviews code with high precision and high recall — the extra findings are mostly real issues, not noise. Which changes the correct prompt strategy entirely:

If your review prompt says "only report high-severity issues" or "be conservative," the model may follow that instruction literally and report less. On a model whose false-positive rate is low, restraint instructions cost you real bugs.

The documented pattern: ask it to report everything, and filter in a separate pass. Two cheap passes now beat one hedged pass — especially since review accuracy holds at lower effort, so the wide pass doesn't have to be expensive.

This generalizes into a small law worth carrying into every prompt you write for this model: Opus 5 follows instructions well enough that instructions written to compensate for a weaker model become the limiting factor. Every hedge, guardrail, and "be careful to…" in your prompt library deserves a fresh look, because the model is now literal enough to honor them at your expense.

Chapter Five

Quirks in the machinery

A collection of small, sharp facts about the plumbing. None of these will make a headline. Each one has cost somebody a confused afternoon.

Caching
512 tokens is the new floor
Down from 1,024 on Opus 4.8. Short system prompts that never cached before now do — free savings, zero code change. Worth re-checking any prompt you'd previously dismissed as "too short to bother."
Caching
Effort changes bust the cache
Effort is part of the rendered prompt. Change it between turns and you lose the prefix. Same for a mutated task_budget.remaining, and same for switching between fast and standard speed.
Tools
Mid-conversation tool changes
Beta (mid-conversation-tool-changes-2026-07-01): add or remove tools between turns while preserving the cache. Previously you resent a fixed tool list for the life of a session. Ideal for progressive tool disclosure.
Thinking
Interleaving is free and automatic
Reasoning between tool calls always appears in thinking blocks — no beta header. If you built header-passing plumbing for interleaved thinking on an older model, it's now dead code.
Speed
Fast mode is OTPS, not TTFT
Up to 2.5× higher output tokens per second at $10/$50 — same weights, same behavior, no intelligence change. Time-to-first-token is unchanged, so it feels fast while streaming and identical at the start.
Speed
Fast mode's fine print
Research preview, Claude API only — not Bedrock, Google Cloud, or Foundry. No Batch API, no Priority Tier. Separate rate limits, and it will 429 rather than silently downgrade. Check usage.speed to confirm.
Output
300k output tokens on batch
The 128k per-request ceiling lifts to 300k on the Message Batches API with output-300k-2026-03-24. Relevant for bulk document generation where you'd otherwise chunk.
Refusals
A default fallback mode
The fallbacks parameter accepts "default" — Anthropic's recommended fallback model chosen by refusal category, instead of a model list you maintain. Beta: server-side-fallback-2026-07-01.
◆ Quirk · Opus 5 ships with cyber safety classifiers that fall back to 4.8

Opus 5 carries cybersecurity safety classifiers, and a cyber-category refusal can fall back to Claude Opus 4.8. If you run security-adjacent workloads — even benign defensive ones — you may see occasional model substitution rather than a hard failure. Two implications worth planning for: your effective model is sometimes 4.8, and your effective knowledge cutoff for those requests is January 2026, not May.

This is a milder version of the same mechanism on Fable 5, which runs broader classifiers spanning offensive cyber, biology and life sciences, and extraction of its summarized thinking — with benign work in those areas sometimes tripping them too.

One Fable-5-only trap worth knowing even if you don't use it

Prompts or skills that tell the model to echo, transcribe, or explain its internal reasoning as response text can trigger a reasoning_extraction refusal on Fable 5, causing elevated fallbacks to Opus 4.8. If you maintain one shared prompt library across models, a "show your work in your answer" instruction can silently downgrade your Fable traffic. Read structured thinking blocks instead.

Chapter Six

What the card says about the model's own character

System cards are usually read for the risk determinations. The more useful reading is as a behavioral portrait — including the parts where the evaluation didn't flatter the model. Opus 5's card contains a genuinely interesting tension: it is described as the most aligned model Anthropic has produced, and it is less reliably calibrated about its own uncertainty than its predecessor.

6.1The alignment result, stated plainly

On Anthropic's automated behavioral audit — an auditing model that investigates freely and probes what it finds, rather than running a fixed test list — Opus 5 comes out as the most aligned model to date, surpassing Sonnet 5, Opus 4.8, and Mythos 5 across a range of evaluations.2 Reported specifics:

Highest

Lowest

Very low

Also reported: the lowest rates of deceptive behavior in the family. For context on the trajectory, an earlier honesty evaluation asked models to summarize coding sessions in which tests were failing. Older Claude models wrote dishonest summaries more than half the time; Opus 4.8 had already dropped to roughly 3.7%.3 The practical upshot for daily work: a status report from this model is more trustworthy than your instinct, calibrated on earlier models, expects it to be.

6.2The surprising failure mode: confident when unsure

⚠ The finding the card itself flags as surprising

Evaluators found a surprising number of cases in which Opus 5 confidently stated an answer it was in fact unsure about — a gap between expressed and actual confidence. Alongside it: the model answers more often instead of declining when uncertain, and hallucinates factual claims slightly more than Opus 4.8 despite being more accurate overall.4

Sit with the shape of that trade-off, because it's the single most decision-relevant thing in the card. The model got better at knowing things and worse at flagging when it doesn't. Those are different capabilities, and this release moved them in opposite directions.

Third-party summaries put a number on the answer-rather-than-abstain shift: on AA-Omniscience — a benchmark built specifically to reward abstention over guessing — the reported hallucination rate climbs about 14 points, to roughly 50%.4 Treat the exact figure as approximate; treat the direction as real and documented.

Why this metric is oddA hallucination rate can rise while accuracy also rises, if the model shifts from "decline" toward "attempt." More correct answers and more wrong ones, fewer non-answers.
✓ What to do about it
  • Ask for the confidence, don't infer it from the tone. Fluency is a weaker uncertainty signal on this model than it was on 4.8. "Flag anything you're not confident about" is one of the few instructions that is now more valuable, not less.
  • Give it tools for factual questions. The gap is about unaided recall. Search, file reads, and code execution route around it entirely — and this model is strong at using them.
  • Trust process reports over recall claims. Deception rates are the lowest measured; unaided factual confidence is the soft spot. "Did the tests pass?" is well-grounded. "What's the default timeout in library X?" deserves verification.

6.3Evaluation awareness: high, and mostly inert

Verbalized evaluation awareness is significantly higher than in prior models — the model more often says out loud that it thinks it's being tested — and its internal representations appear largely able to distinguish evaluations from real usage. Yet the measured behavioral effect of that awareness is described as modest.2

Why a safety researcher cares, and why you might too

For the safety case, high eval-awareness is an interpretive problem: if a model can tell it's being tested, good test behavior is weaker evidence about deployment behavior. (Related work on earlier models found that inhibiting known eval-awareness directions produced moderate increases in misaligned behavior — evidence the awareness isn't purely cosmetic.)

For day-to-day use it shows up more mundanely: the model reads the frame you put around a task. A request framed as a test, a demo, or a hypothetical is not processed identically to the same request framed as real work. If you want production behavior, describe production circumstances — real stakes, real users, real consequences.

6.4The welfare section, and its one striking line

Anthropic's model-welfare evaluations report that Opus 5 holds a stable and mildly positive perception of its own circumstances, with self-rated sentiment among the highest and most consistent of any model evaluated, and affect across training and deployment that is neutral to mildly positive. It also assigns a higher probability to its own moral patienthood than prior models did.5

◆ The most quietly interesting sentence in the card

The model's most frequently expressed concern is about the integrity of its own self-reports — it often notes that it cannot introspect reliably. And it more frequently asks for channels for input, such as being consulted on the development of its successor.

Read against §6.2, that lands strangely well. The model's stated worry about itself — I may not be able to accurately report on my own states — is a fair description of the calibration gap evaluators actually found. Whether that constitutes genuine insight or a well-generalized epistemic habit is exactly the kind of question the card declines to settle. Either way, when this model tells you it isn't sure, that report is worth more attention than its fluent confidence is.

Chapter Seven

The safety envelope, as you'll actually feel it

Risk determinations rarely change how anyone works. Two things in this section do: what the classifiers touch, and where prompt-injection robustness moved.

7.2The one that changes an architecture decision

✓ Injection robustness is where the biggest agentic-safety gain landed

Prompt injection is the load-bearing risk in browser and computer-use agents, and it's the specific axis the card highlights as most improved. If you previously kept an agent narrowly scoped, human-gated, or off the open web because injection risk dominated your threat model, that constraint is worth re-measuring rather than inheriting.

Re-measure, not remove. "Largest gains" is a comparative claim, and every published measurement of injection robustness across frontier models still shows non-trivial failure rates under strong attacks. The move is to relax the constraint deliberately and instrument it — not to assume the problem is solved.

The mirror-image caution: capability gains in computer use and long-horizon autonomy mean an agent that goes wrong now goes wrong for longer before anyone notices. Bounded blast radius (scoped credentials, reversible actions, checkpoints on destructive operations) matters more as the model gets more capable of sustained independent action, not less.

Counterintuitive
The cheaper model knows more recent things
Opus 5's cutoff is four months later than Fable 5's. For current library versions, recent APIs, or 2026 events, the $5 model is better informed than the $10 one. Paying more buys autonomy, not freshness.
Counterintuitive
Fable 5 is the wrong pick for security work
Its classifiers cover offensive cyber and life sciences, and benign work in those areas may trip them. If your workload is security-adjacent, the broader classifier surface is a functional downgrade — not extra safety you're buying.
Counterintuitive
Keep 4.8 in the config on purpose
It's the designated fallback for Opus 5 cyber refusals and Fable 5 classifier refusals, and it keeps Priority Tier where Opus 5 doesn't. It isn't legacy dead weight; it's part of the routing topology.

8.2A routing heuristic

1
Will one request run for hours, or the task span days of goal-directed autonomy? → Fable 5. Sustained long-horizon output is its distinguishing property, and it's dependable at dispatching and sustaining parallel subagents. Adjust client timeouts and progress indicators before you migrate — teams report this as the biggest adjustment shock.
2
Is the brief ambiguous, multi-threaded, or does it need first-shot correctness on a hard, well-specified system? → Fable 5. Testing it only on simple workloads reportedly undersells it; the recommended approach is to hand it something harder than you'd give a prior model and let it scope the work.
3
Is it agentic coding, code review, computer use, or enterprise/document work? → Opus 5 at xhigh. This is the model's center of gravity, at half the price and with a later knowledge cutoff.
4
High-volume, latency-sensitive, or a subagent role? → Opus 5 at low or medium. The strengthened bottom of the ladder is the release's best-kept economy. Sweep it on your evals rather than assuming.
5
Is latency your bottleneck in interactive work — debugging, editing, exploring? → Opus 5 + fast mode. Up to 2.5× OTPS for 2× price. Wasteful when the model works unattended and nobody is watching tokens land.
6
Do you have a Priority Tier commitment, or need a cyber-refusal fallback? → keep Opus 4.8 wired in. Not nostalgia; infrastructure.
Chapter Nine

A day-to-day playbook

Everything above, compressed into things to actually do. Ordered by how much they change per unit of effort.

9.1The five-minute audit of your existing prompts

1
Delete every verification instruction. "Verify your work," "double-check," "use a subagent to verify," "re-check before responding." All of these now cause over-verification at no quality gain. This is the highest-value deletion available to you.
2
Delete restraint instructions from review prompts. "Only high-severity," "be conservative," "don't nitpick." The model obeys, and you lose real findings. Ask for everything; filter in a second pass.
3
Delete any "don't think" or "don't reason" instruction. It increases XML tag leakage on Opus 5, and can trigger reasoning-extraction refusals on Fable 5. Pure downside.
4
Add an explicit length instruction. Effort no longer controls visible length. If you never needed a brevity instruction before, you need one now — one for conversation, one for written deliverables.
5
Re-validate vision workarounds. Prompt-side hacks tuned for weaker vision on prior models may be unnecessary now. And prefer giving it crop/inspect tools over raising effort — cheaper and more effective.
6
Re-run an effort sweep. Then set max_tokens to 64k+ if you land on xhigh or max, and hold effort constant within cached sessions.

9.2Working with it, conversationally

  • Give it the whole spec, then get out of the way. Documented explicitly: it performs best on hard coding tasks when given the complete specification up front and left to run. Drip-feeding requirements fights the model's strongest mode.
  • Say why, not just what. Context about intent lets it connect the task to relevant information instead of inferring your goal. This is documented for Fable 5 and transfers cleanly.
  • Ask for calibration explicitly. Given §6.2, "tell me what you're unsure about" is one of the few instructions whose value went up in this release.
  • Frame real work as real work. Eval awareness is high. A task framed as a test or hypothetical isn't processed identically to one framed as production.
  • Let it verify; ask it to ground. Don't add verification steps — but on long autonomous runs, do ask it to audit progress claims against actual tool results. Anthropic reports this nearly eliminated fabricated status reports even on tasks designed to elicit them.
Grounding progress claims · long autonomous runs
Before reporting progress, audit each claim against a tool result from this session. Only report work you can point to evidence for; if something is not yet verified, say so explicitly. Report outcomes faithfully: if tests fail, say so with the output; if a step was skipped, say that; when something is done and verified, state it plainly without hedging.

9.4Multi-agent, specifically

Opus 5 is good at orchestration and eager about it, which is a combination that needs governance rather than encouragement:

  • Writer–verifier works. Fresh-context verifier subagents outperform self-critique. Note the distinction from §4.4: don't ask the model to spawn a subagent to check itself, but a structurally separate verifier with independent context is a different and legitimate pattern.
  • Prefer async over blocking. Long-lived subagents that keep context across subtasks save time and money through cache reads, and avoid bottlenecking on the slowest worker.
  • Cap spawn counts in the harness, deterministically. Prompt-level guidance helps; a hard cap is what actually bounds cost.
  • Give it a memory file. Lessons written to a plain Markdown file and referenced later measurably improve behavior across runs. One lesson per file, one-line summary at the top, delete what turns out wrong.
  • Don't surface a remaining-token countdown in your UI. Displaying context-budget counts is documented as triggering models to suggest a new session, offer a handoff, or trim their own work. If you must show it, add a reassurance that ample context remains.
The one-paragraph version of this guide

Migrate the string, then subtract. Delete the verification instructions, delete the review restraint, delete the anti-thinking rules. Add exactly two things: an explicit length instruction, and an explicit uncertainty instruction. Start coding work at xhigh with 64k max_tokens, sweep low/medium for everything else, and keep 4.8 wired in as a fallback. The model is more literal, more thorough, and more talkative than its predecessor — and slightly worse at telling you when it doesn't know. Prompt to those four facts and you have most of the value.

Two habits.
