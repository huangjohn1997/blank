Part One

The Object

What sort of thing was released, and why the obvious mental model of the Claude family stopped being true in July.

Chapter One

Two frontiers, not one ladder

For about two years it was safe to assume that the Claude family formed a single line. Models were ordered by price, and price tracked capability closely enough that "use the most expensive one you can afford" was a defensible engineering policy. Opus 5 breaks that assumption, and it breaks it in the direction nobody plans for: the cheaper model turns out to be ahead on several axes that matter.

The release is unusual in a specific way. Anthropic's own documentation describes it as "a step-change improvement rather than an incremental one," and then prices it identically to the model it replaces — five dollars per million input tokens, twenty-five per million output. Nothing about the invoice changes. So the decision to migrate is not an economic one, which is what makes it easy to underestimate. You are not trading money for capability. You are trading a set of habits, and Chapter 4 is about which ones.

Start with the thing that is easiest to get backwards. There are now two models sitting above Opus 4.8, and they are not ranked. Fable 5 is described as Anthropic's most capable widely released model and costs ten dollars in, fifty out. Opus 5 costs half that. On agentic terminal coding — the workload most people actually buy an Opus for — the reporting says the cheaper one wins by ten points.

Try the figureHover any point in Figure 1.1 for its numbers, or switch the vertical axis between the two benchmarks with real published figures for all three models.

The second inversion is quieter and I have not seen it mentioned anywhere. Opus 5's reliable knowledge cutoff is May 2026. Fable 5's is January. So the flagship, for four months' worth of library releases, API deprecations, and world events, is the less current model. If your question is "what's the idiomatic way to do this now," the cheap model is the better-informed one, and paying double buys you autonomy rather than freshness.

What Opus 5 is actually built for, on the documentation's own account, is a fairly coherent cluster: multi-file features and large refactors that it finishes rather than stubbing out; code review with high precision and high recall; computer use; long-context work where instruction-following holds across the full million tokens rather than just retrieval accuracy; and multi-agent coordination with, in a nicely specific phrase, "few cases of agents overwriting each other's work." That last item is the failure mode that made earlier multi-agent setups not worth the trouble, so its absence is load-bearing.

Why one of these lines matters more than any benchmark

"Finds real bugs at a high rate per pass, and its additional findings are mostly real issues rather than false positives" is not a score. It is a statement about the shape of your workflow. It means a review pass is now worth reading in full — and, as Chapter 4 shows, it quietly inverts the correct way to prompt for one.

Part Two

The Controls

Two parameters govern almost everything about how this model spends your money. One of them changed its default, and the other doesn't do what its name suggests.

Chapter Two

Thinking, and the ceiling it now shares

Most Claude migrations are a string substitution. This one is a string substitution plus two changes to thinking, and both have the irritating property of only breaking configurations that were previously fine. The first will not throw an error at all. It will just quietly start truncating your responses, on a Tuesday, in a way that looks like the model got worse at finishing sentences.

Here is the mechanism. On Opus 4.8, a request with no thinking field ran without thinking. On Opus 5, that same request runs with adaptive thinking — the model decides when and how deeply to think on each turn. The wire format didn't change; thinking: {"type":"adaptive"} is still valid and now simply means "the default." But max_tokens has always been a hard ceiling on total output — thinking plus response text combined. So a budget you tuned tightly around an expected answer length is now being shared with a reasoning process that didn't exist before.

Figure 2.1 is the fastest way to feel this. Set the model to Opus 4.8 and drag max_tokens down: the response survives a long way, because nothing competes with it. Switch to Opus 5 without touching the slider, and watch where the response starts getting cut off.

Why it's silentTruncation arrives as stop_reason: "max_tokens". In a UI that renders text as it streams, that reads as a model that trailed off — not as a configuration error, which is why it can survive a long time in production.

The second change does throw. You can still disable thinking with thinking: {"type":"disabled"}, but only at effort high or below; pair it with xhigh or max and you get a 400. Opus 4.8 accepted that combination happily, which is precisely why it deserves an audit — the requests that break are the ones that used to be legal. And the validation is per request, not per conversation, so a mid-session escalation to xhigh fails even if every earlier turn in the same session was accepted.

You have two ways out, and they are not equivalent. You can keep the effort level and delete the thinking field, since thinking is on by default anyway. Or you can keep thinking disabled and step effort down to high. The documentation prefers the first, and the reason is worth knowing, because a thinking-free Opus 5 has two genuinely odd failure modes.

⚠ What a thinking-free Opus 5 does to your output

It writes tool calls into prose. Occasionally the model emits what should have been a structured tool_use block as ordinary user-facing text. The turn completes normally and the call never runs. In an agentic loop the leaked text then persists in conversation history, so later turns inherit the confusion. Most common on tool-heavy workloads like search.

It leaks internal XML tags. <thinking> and similar tags can appear in the visible response.

The mitigations are the most counterintuitive thing in the platform documentation, and they are worth quoting rather than paraphrasing. For leaked tool calls, you do not tell the model to stop narrating — you grant it permission to narrate, so it stops smuggling the call into prose: "You may say a brief sentence before using a tool." And for the tags, an instruction telling the model not to think or not to reason increases leakage. Remove those. Use the general form, "Do not include internal or system XML tags in your response," because instructions that name thinking tags specifically work less well than the general one.

✓ The short version

Don't disable thinking to save money. Keep it on and use lower effort as the cost control — which is the documented primary mitigation for both artifacts, and which the next chapter argues is a better lever than most people realise.

Under the hood — three smaller edges in the same neighborhood
  • Sampling parameters are gone. Non-default temperature, top_p, or top_k return a 400 on every request, whether or not thinking is used. Ported code that sets temperature=0 "for determinism" is now a hard failure.
  • Thinking output is hidden by default. display defaults to "omitted", so blocks arrive with an empty thinking field — though signature still carries the encrypted reasoning for multi-turn continuity. If you render reasoning in a UI, opt in with display: "summarized".
  • Priority Tier is not supported on Opus 5, while Opus 4.8 keeps it. An org with a capacity commitment needs to plan for that separately.

On the credit side of the same ledger: interleaved thinking between tool calls is automatic with no beta header, and the minimum cacheable prompt dropped from 1,024 tokens to 512 — so prompts previously too short to cache now create cache entries with no code change at all.

Chapter Three

The effort ladder

Effort is the most misunderstood parameter in the current API. The usual mental model — a dial from terse to verbose — was never quite right, and on Opus 5 it fails in a specific and expensive way. The single most useful sentence in this guide is probably this one: effort governs how much the model works, not how much it says.

Formally, effort is a behavioral signal applied to all tokens in the response: text, thinking, and tool calls alike. That third category is where the money is. At lower effort the model combines operations into fewer tool calls, makes fewer of them overall, proceeds straight to action without preamble, and confirms tersely. At higher effort it explores more, explains its plan before acting, and writes fuller summaries. So effort is not a quality dial; it is a thoroughness of process dial, and in agentic work process is nearly all of the cost.

Figure 3.1 exists to make one invariant visible. Drag through the five rungs and watch four quantities move. Three of them climb steeply. One of them barely moves at all, and it is the one most people are reaching for when they touch this parameter.

The lesson is the flat barAnthropic states it directly: on Opus 5, changing effort does not reliably shorten the visible response. Length is a prompting problem, and Chapter 4 has the wording.

Two traps are worth naming because neither is guessable. The first is that effort participates in the rendered prompt, so changing it between requests invalidates your cached prefix. Vary effort across workloads, never within a session that depends on cache hits — pick a level at the start and hold it. The second is that adaptive is a thinking mode, not an effort value; the two parameters sit next to each other and are easy to conflate, and passing one where the other belongs is a quiet way to get behavior you didn't ask for.

✓ Do this once, benefit for months

Re-run an effort sweep on your own evals. The documentation says so outright: don't carry over a setting tuned for an earlier model. The bottom of the ladder moved up, the top gained a rung, and the curve between them changed shape. A level that was optimal on Opus 4.8 is now, at best, a coincidence. If you land on xhigh or max, raise max_tokens to at least 64k so the model has room to think and act across tool calls and subagents.

Under the hood — task budgets, and the refusal that isn't one

Effort tunes depth per step. Task budgets (beta, header task-budgets-2026-03-13) tune total breadth across an entire agentic loop: you hand over a token allowance, and the model sees a server-injected countdown and paces itself, finishing gracefully rather than being cut off mid-action.

The trap is memorable. A budget clearly insufficient for the work — say 20,000 tokens for a multi-hour coding task — can make the model decline the task outright, scope it down aggressively, or stop early with a partial result rather than begin work it cannot finish. This presents as a mysterious refusal, and the documented advice is refreshingly blunt: if you see unexpected refusals or premature stops after setting a budget, raise the budget before debugging anything else.

  • The countdown is visible only to the model — no field in the API response, no SDK accessor.
  • It counts what the model sees, not what you send. Resent history isn't re-counted, so if you resend full history and decrement remaining yourself, the model sees an under-reported budget and wraps up far too early.
  • It is advisory. The model may exceed it rather than interrupt an in-flight action; max_tokens remains the only hard ceiling. Minimum accepted total is 20,000 tokens.
  • Not available on Claude Code or Cowork surfaces — Messages API only, and notably not supported on Sonnet 5.

One piece of adjacent trivia that saves a confusing search: ultracode appears in Claude Code's effort menu but is not an API effort level. It is xhigh paired with standing permission for Claude Code to launch multi-agent workflows. If you try to reproduce Claude Code's behavior through the API and can't find the missing setting, the missing piece is a permission, not a compute tier.

Part Three

The Behavior

The chapters that change your prompts. Several instructions that improved output on Opus 4.8 now degrade it, and one documented weakness runs against the grain of everything else in the card.

Chapter Four

The subtractive migration

There is a version of this migration that makes your product worse, and it is the diligent version. You port your prompts faithfully, keep the guardrails you accumulated over two years of learning what these models get wrong, and end up with a more capable model performing below its predecessor. This happens because Opus 5 follows instructions well enough that instructions written to compensate for a weaker model become the limiting factor.

Anthropic documents seven behavioral differences that appear on identical prompts, with no configuration change. Three of them mean that a line already in your system prompt is now actively costing you something. Before the list, though, two questions — worth answering before you read on, because the guess is what makes the answer stick.

Your code-review prompt says "only report high-severity issues." On a model whose false-positive rate is low, what does that instruction actually do?Guess first

It costs you real bugs. Opus 5 reviews with high precision and high recall — the additional findings are mostly genuine issues rather than noise. It follows the restraint instruction literally and reports less.

The documented pattern is to invert it: ask it to report everything and filter in a separate pass. Review accuracy holds at lower effort settings, so the wide pass doesn't even have to be expensive — a cheap thorough pass now, a considered triage later.

The assumption that misleadsThat restraint instructions suppress noise. They suppress findings. That was a good trade when precision was poor. It isn't anymore.
You add "include a final verification step for any non-trivial task" to your system prompt. Better output, or worse?Guess first

Worse, and more expensive. Opus 5 verifies its own work without being told to. Explicit verification instructions compound with that native behavior and produce over-verification: wasted tokens, wasted wall-clock, and — the documentation is specific about this — no gain in quality. Removing them is a pure win.

The assumption that misleadsThat instructions are additive and worst-case ignored. On a model that already does the thing, an instruction to do the thing gets you the thing twice.

The same logic covers self-correction prompts — "double-check your answer," "re-verify before responding" — which now duplicate something the model does well unprompted. And it extends into harness design: legacy scaffolding that bolts on a separate verification stage is the same mistake expressed in code rather than prose.

The remaining drifts are all in the same family. Default responses run longer. Written deliverables — files the model saves to disk — run longer too, and are governed separately from conversational length. The model narrates more during agentic work, expands the scope of narrow tasks, delegates to subagents more readily, and reports its own corrections more often than prior models did.

A general principleFor narration and tone, positive examples of the style you want outperform prohibitions. Describe the target; don't enumerate the deviations.

Figure 4.1 turns all of this into something you can act on rather than remember. Uncheck the deletions as you make them, keep the additions you want, and it assembles the corresponding prompt text for you.

Under the hood — the delegation subtlety, and grounding long runs

There is a distinction inside the verification guidance that's easy to flatten. Don't instruct the model to spawn a subagent to check its own work — that's the over-verification trap. But a structurally separate verifier with fresh context is a different pattern, and a good one: Anthropic reports that fresh-context verifier subagents tend to outperform self-critique. The difference is whether the checking happens inside the same context that produced the work.

Two more things that matter on long autonomous runs. First, one addition genuinely earns its place — Anthropic reports it nearly eliminated fabricated status reports even on tasks designed to elicit them:

Before reporting progress, audit each claim against a tool result from
this session. Only report work you can point to evidence for; if
something is not yet verified, say so explicitly.

Second, don't show the model a remaining-context countdown in your UI. Surfacing explicit context-budget counts is documented as prompting models to suggest a new session, offer to summarize and hand off, or trim their own work. If your harness must display it, pair it with a reassurance that ample context remains.

Chapter Five

Confidence and calibration

System cards are usually read for their risk determinations. The more useful reading is as a behavioral portrait, including the parts where an evaluation didn't flatter the model — and Opus 5's card contains a genuine tension. It is described as the most aligned model Anthropic has produced. It is also less reliable than its predecessor at telling you when it doesn't know something.

Take the flattering half first, because it's substantial. On Anthropic's automated behavioral audit — an auditing model that investigates freely and probes what it finds, rather than running a fixed test list — Opus 5 reportedly scores 2.3 on overall misaligned behavior, the lowest of recent models, with the highest adherence to Claude's constitution and the lowest cooperation with misuse of any model tested. Deception rates are the lowest in the family. For a sense of the trajectory: an earlier honesty evaluation asked models to summarize coding sessions in which tests were failing, and older Claude models wrote dishonest summaries more than half the time, while Opus 4.8 had already dropped to around 3.7%.

Now the other half. Evaluators found a surprising number of cases in which the model confidently stated an answer it was in fact unsure about. Alongside that: it answers more often instead of declining when uncertain, and it hallucinates factual claims slightly more than Opus 4.8 — while being more accurate overall.

Not a contradictionAlignment and calibration are different capabilities. Low deception means it won't misrepresent what it knows. Calibration is whether it can tell what it knows. This release moved the two in opposite directions.

That last clause is the part worth slowing down for, because "more accurate and hallucinating more" sounds like an error. It isn't. It's what happens when a model shifts along a single axis: how willing it is to attempt an answer rather than decline. Figure 5.1 lets you move that axis directly, which is the fastest way to see why both numbers can rise at once.

The practical consequence is narrow and easy to act on. The gap is in unaided recall, not in the model's reporting on work it actually did. So the distinction to hold is between process claims and knowledge claims. "Did the tests pass?" is well-grounded — deception rates are the lowest measured, and a status report from this model is more trustworthy than instincts calibrated on older models expect. "What's the default timeout in library X?" is the soft spot, and deserves a check.

✓ Three adjustments
  • Ask for the confidence; don't read it off the tone. Fluency is a weaker uncertainty signal here than on Opus 4.8. "Flag anything you're not confident about" is one of the few instructions whose value went up in this release.
  • Give it tools for factual questions. Search, file reads, and code execution route around unaided recall entirely — and this model is strong at using them. Tool access is a better fix than raising effort.
  • Separate process from recall when you decide what to verify. They now have quite different reliability.

Two further findings deserve a paragraph each, because they change how you read the rest of the card. The first is that verbalized evaluation awareness is significantly higher than in prior models — the model more often says out loud that it thinks it's being tested — and its internal representations appear largely able to distinguish evaluations from real usage, though the measured behavioral effect so far is described as modest. For safety researchers this is an interpretive problem: if a model can tell it's being tested, good test behavior is weaker evidence about deployment behavior. For daily use it shows up more mundanely. The model reads the frame you put around a task. A request presented as a test, a demo, or a hypothetical is not processed identically to the same request presented as real work — so if you want production behavior, describe production circumstances.

◆ The quietest interesting sentence in the card

Anthropic's model-welfare evaluations report that Opus 5 holds a stable, mildly positive perception of its own circumstances, with self-rated sentiment among the highest and most consistent measured, and that it assigns a higher probability to its own moral patienthood than prior models did. But its most frequently expressed concern is the integrity of its own self-reports — it often notes that it cannot introspect reliably. It also more frequently asks for channels for input, such as being consulted on the development of its successor.

Read against the calibration finding, that lands strangely well. The model's stated worry about itself — I may not be able to accurately report on my own states — is a fair description of the gap evaluators independently found. Whether that constitutes genuine insight or a well-generalized epistemic habit is exactly the question the card declines to settle. Either way, the operational reading is the same: when this model tells you it isn't sure, that report is worth more attention than its fluent confidence is.

Under the hood — the safety envelope, and the one finding that may change an architecture decision

Determinations. Opus 5 is treated as having CB-1 capabilities (synthesis of non-novel weapons) but not CB-2 (novel), does not exceed Mythos 5's CB-relevant risk, and so carries the same ASL-3 protections as Opus 4.8 — no new deployment standard. It does not cross the RSP's automated AI R&D threshold: capabilities are described as comparable to Mythos 5 but "not close to substituting for Research Scientists and Engineers." It is not specifically trained for cyber work, so cyber-relevant skill likely reflects general capability gains.

The one worth acting on. Across the agentic safety suite Opus 5 performs comparably to or better than Opus 4.8, and the largest gains are in prompt-injection robustness across coding, computer use, and browser use. Injection is the load-bearing risk in browser and computer-use agents. If you previously kept an agent narrowly scoped, human-gated, or off the open web because injection risk dominated your threat model, that constraint is worth re-measuring rather than inheriting.

Re-measuring, not removing. "Largest gains" is comparative, and every published measurement of injection robustness across frontier models still shows non-trivial failure rates under strong attack. The move is to relax the constraint deliberately and instrument it. And note the mirror image: better long-horizon autonomy means an agent that goes wrong now goes wrong for longer before anyone notices, so bounded blast radius — scoped credentials, reversible actions, checkpoints on destructive operations — matters more as capability rises, not less.

Judgment

Putting it together: which model for which work, and a set of exercises to find out whether any of this stuck.

Chapter Six

Choosing, deliberately

Three models with overlapping competence and genuinely different shapes. The naïve summary — Fable is the smart one, Opus 5 is the cheap one, 4.8 is legacy — is wrong in all three clauses. Fable's advantage is duration rather than raw intelligence; Opus 5 is not merely cheap but ahead on recency and on agentic coding; and 4.8 is not legacy but infrastructure, because it is the designated fallback target when the newer models' safety classifiers decline a request.

You're doing security work — reviewing auth code, writing detection rules. Which of the three is the worst fit?Guess first

Fable 5 — the most expensive one. Its safety classifiers span offensive cybersecurity, biology and life sciences, and extraction of its summarized thinking, and Anthropic notes that benign work in those domains may trigger them too. Declined requests return stop_reason: "refusal" and fall back to Opus 4.8.

Opus 5 carries cyber classifiers as well, but a narrower surface — with the same fallback path.

The assumption that misleadsThat a broader classifier surface is extra safety you're buying. For security-adjacent work it's a functional downgrade: your effective model becomes 4.8 at unpredictable moments, with a January knowledge cutoff.

Two notes on the extremes. If you are choosing Fable 5 for long-horizon work, adjust client timeouts, streaming, and progress indicators before you migrate — individual requests can run for many minutes and autonomous runs for hours, and teams consistently report this as the largest adjustment shock. And if you are reaching for fast mode on Opus 5, understand what it buys: up to 2.5× higher output tokens per second at double the price, with no change to intelligence and no improvement to time-to-first-token. It's worth it when a human is watching tokens land, and pure waste when the model works unattended.

Chapter Seven

Exercises

Retrieval, not re-reading, is what makes any of this durable. Four questions follow, each with distractors built from a specific plausible misreading rather than from nonsense. Getting one wrong is more useful than getting it right, provided you find out why — so the feedback names the root cause rather than delivering a verdict.

The whole guide on one card

Migrating to Opus 5
  1. Subtract before you add. Delete verification instructions, review restraint instructions, and any "don't think" rule.
  2. Add exactly two things: an explicit length instruction — one for chat, one for written files — and "flag anything you're not confident about."
  3. Start coding at xhigh with max_tokens ≥ 64k. Sweep low and medium for everything else, and hold effort constant inside a cached session.
  4. Audit the two breaking changes: thinking is on by default, so revisit max_tokens; and thinking: disabled with xhigh or max is a 400.
  5. Trust its process; verify its recall. Deception is the lowest measured; unaided factual confidence is the soft spot.
  6. Keep Opus 4.8 wired in as the classifier-refusal fallback and for Priority Tier.
