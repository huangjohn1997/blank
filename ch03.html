Volume I · Part I · Chapter 3 — Prediction and representation

From IDs to learned vectors

The tokenizer hands the model integers. Integers cannot express similarity. This chapter installs the one piece of machinery that can — and it turns out to be a table you look things up in.

§3.1 The need

An ID is an address, not a quantity

Our tokenizer assigns ▁the some number and ▁keeper some other number. Suppose they are 312 and 87. The model receives 312. What can it do with that?

It can compare it to other numbers, but every comparison is meaningless: 312 is not “more” than 87 in any sense the language supports, and the token whose ID is 313 has no relationship to the token whose ID is 312 — they are neighbours by accident of merge order. It can multiply it by a weight, but then the strength of that weight’s effect scales with an arbitrary label, which is worse than meaningless: it is actively misleading.

Before fixing this, commit to a prediction. The question below is the one that separates people who have understood this from people who have memorised the phrase “embeddings are learned”.

§3.2 The mechanism

One row per token

The fix is almost embarrassingly plain. Build a table with one row per vocabulary entry and d numbers in each row. To turn a token ID into something computable, fetch its row. That row is the token’s , the table is the embedding matrix E, and its shape is V × d — vocabulary size by model dimension.

Two vocabulary items now occupy d-dimensional space, so questions that were unaskable become askable: how far apart are they, do they point the same way, what lies between them. We have not yet said anything about what the numbers should be. That comes in §3.4, and it comes from the prediction loss of Chapter 1, not from a dictionary.

Textbooks and papers usually present the lookup as a matrix multiplication, and it is worth seeing why, because the notation appears everywhere. Write the token as a one-hot vector: a row of V numbers, all zero except a single 1 at the token’s position. Multiply that by E.

Equation 3.1 · Selecting a row is a matrix multiplication in disguise
ei⊤ ⁢ E = Ei,: (1×V)⁢(V×d)=(1×d)
Read it as: a vector that is all zeros except a 1 in position i, multiplied by the table, returns exactly row i — every other row is multiplied by zero and vanishes. The shapes on the right confirm it: one token in, d numbers out. No implementation does this multiplication. They index the row directly, because multiplying by all those zeros is pure waste.

§3.3 First consequence

What the table costs

The table is V × d numbers, and every one of them is a — a number training is free to change and inference must keep in memory.

Equation 3.2 · The size of the embedding table, and what it occupies
Nemb= V·d bytes= V·d· b
Read it as: vocabulary size times model dimension gives the parameter count; multiply by the bytes per number to get memory. b is 4 for 32-bit floats, 2 for the 16-bit formats used in most training, and 1 when a model is quantised to 8-bit for serving.

What to notice: the embedding table is a rounding error in a very large model and a substantial fraction of a small one, which is why small multilingual models feel the vocabulary decision most acutely. And the last panel shows the part people forget: with realistic word frequencies, most of the rows are trained on almost nothing.

That last point is worth stating plainly, because it connects two chapters. Chapter 1 showed that word frequencies follow a heavy tail: a few tokens appear constantly, most appear rarely. Every row of E is updated only when its token appears. So the table’s rows are not equally trained — the frequent rows are shaped by millions of examples and the tail rows by a handful. When a model behaves strangely on a rare name, a technical term, or a low-resource language, one honest hypothesis is that the relevant rows are simply undertrained. Inferred — the mechanism is certain, the attribution in any particular case needs evidence.

§3.4 The second mechanism

Where the numbers come from

The rows start as small random numbers. They are then changed by exactly the process Chapter 1 defined: make a prediction, measure the surprise, and adjust every parameter a little in the direction that would have reduced it. That is , and Chapter 8 builds it properly. What matters here is a structural detail with large consequences.

When the model reads token i, the only row of E that touched the computation is row i. A parameter that did not affect the prediction cannot have affected the loss, so its gradient is zero. The embedding update is therefore sparse: one token read, one row changed.

Equation 3.3 · One prediction error edits one row
Ei,: ← Ei,: − η ∂L∂Ei,: ∂L∂Ej,:=0for allj≠i
Read it as: move row i a small step (η, the learning rate) against the direction in which the loss increases — and leave every other row untouched, because they contributed nothing to this prediction. Over a corpus, each row accumulates only the lessons of its own occurrences.

Nothing about “meaning” was supplied; the only instruction the model received was you should have predicted this token here. Everything the table ever knows is accumulated from that instruction, repeated.

§3.5 Second consequence

Why the rows do not stay unrelated

Here is the consequence that makes the next chapter necessary. Two tokens that need to predict the same things receive similar instructions, over and over. If dog and cat are both followed by eating and running and sleeping, then whatever adjustment helps the model predict those continuations after dog also helps after cat. The rows are updated separately, and they are pushed in the same direction.

Nobody encoded that relationship. It is a side effect of sharing a prediction task.

The geometry is a residue of the training task — which is exactly why Chapter 4 can read structure out of it, and exactly why that structure means only what the task and the corpus put there.

§3.6 Evidence

Three eras of the same table

Established The idea of learning a vector per word inside a neural language model is due to Bengio and colleagues in 2003, who trained a network to predict the next word and let the word vectors be parameters of that network. Everything in this chapter is that architecture, with the vocabulary of Chapter 2 in front of it.

Established In the following decade the vectors themselves became the product: methods like word2vec and GloVe were trained specifically to produce reusable word vectors, which were then loaded into other systems as features. That is Chapter 4’s subject, and it is where the geometry was first studied carefully.

Established In current systems the table is back to being an internal layer. The Transformer paper trains input and output embeddings as part of one end-to-end model, and large autoregressive models followed. Nobody ships “the embeddings” of a modern language model as the deliverable; the deliverable is the model, and the table is its first layer.

Bengio et al., “A Neural Probabilistic Language Model” (2003) · Vaswani et al., “Attention Is All You Need” (2017) · Brown et al., “Language Models are Few-Shot Learners” (2020).

Optional · weight tying

A model needs a table to turn tokens into vectors at the input, and a matrix to turn vectors back into scores over the vocabulary at the output. Both are V × d. Some systems use the same matrix for both, which saves V · d parameters and couples the two roles: every input update also changes the output layer. Press and Wolf analysed this in 2017 and found it can improve quality as well as size.

Worth knowing because it is a recurring “is this one thing or two?” question in model cards — and because the answer changes the parameter count you are being quoted.

§3.7 The limit

One row per token, forever

What we have built is real progress: similarity is now expressible, related tokens can share the benefit of training, and nothing in the model depends on the arbitrary integers. But look hard at the shape of the table and two hard limits are visible in it.

The row does not depend on the sentence. Bank gets one row whether the text is about rivers or money. Whatever the table learns about a token with several uses must be a single compromise between them. No amount of training changes this; it is a property of a table with one row per token.

The row does not know where it is. Fetching rows for a sequence gives you a bag of vectors with no order and no interaction. The dog bit the man and the man bit the dog produce the same set of rows.

Those two sentences are the agenda for the rest of Part I. Chapter 5 gives the model a state that carries information along the sequence; Chapter 6 lets positions interact selectively; Chapter 7 assembles the result. But before that machinery, there is a question we can answer right now with the table alone, and it is one of the most consequential in the field: what structure do these rows actually acquire, and what may we conclude from it?

Reconstruct — write it before you read it

Trace the causal chain from “the model mispredicted a token” to “two unrelated words ended up with similar rows”. Four or five sentences, no equations.

A model answer. The model reads a token by fetching that token’s row from the table, and uses those numbers to score every possible next token. If the token that actually followed got too little probability, training adjusts the numbers that contributed to the mistake — including that row — a small step in the direction that would have scored the truth higher. Only that row changes, because no other row took part in the computation. Now consider two different tokens that tend to be followed by the same kinds of continuations: each time either one appears, its row is nudged toward representing “the things that follow me”, and since those things overlap, the two rows are repeatedly nudged in similar directions. After enough occurrences they end up close together, without anyone ever having stated that the two words are related.

Check yourself: does your chain include the reason only one row changes? That sparsity is what makes “each row accumulates the lessons of its own occurrences” true — and therefore what makes the frequency tail of Chapter 1 a training-quality problem.

Carry forward
  • Settled: token IDs are addresses; the embedding table gives each token a row of d learned numbers; the one-hot multiplication is notation for a lookup; the table is V · d parameters and one prediction error edits exactly one row.
  • Consequences to keep: vocabulary size buys fertility with memory; rows are trained as unevenly as language is frequent; shared predictive jobs drag rows together.
  • Unresolved, and load-bearing: we have seen that the rows acquire structure. We have not established what that structure is, how to measure it, or what it entitles anyone to claim.
