Volume I · Part I · Chapter 1 — Prediction and representation

The guessing game

There’s one task underneath all of this. Get the task right and half the weirdness of these systems stops being weird.

The shape of this chapter

§1.1 The problem

You already do this, constantly

Read this and stop at the blank. “He filled the lamp with ___.”

Something showed up before you went looking for it. Probably oil. Maybe kerosene. Almost certainly not Tuesday — and notice that Tuesday isn’t ungrammatical there. It fits the slot fine. It’s just that nothing you know about lamps ever put it in the running.

Now this one. “The keeper ___.”

Nothing shows up. Or rather, a dozen things show up and none of them insists. The blank is genuinely open.

Those two blanks are the whole chapter. A machine that continues text has to handle both with the same equipment, and here’s the catch: it can’t just name its favourite word. In the second case, naming a favourite would be a lie. It would hide the fact that the sentence hasn’t decided yet.

So whatever the machine produces has to carry two things at once. Which words are plausible, and how much the context has narrowed things down.

From a guess to a spread of guesses

The language that does both jobs is probability. Instead of picking one word, the machine hands out a number to every word it knows — its — and those numbers have to add up to 1. That object is a , and producing one is the entire job of a .

That “adds up to 1” rule is doing more work than it looks like. It means the machine can’t be enthusiastic about everything. Belief is a fixed budget. Spending more on oil means spending less on everything else. That’s what makes the output informative instead of just positive, and it’s what lets us grade it later.

Two things follow, and we’ll use both for the next thirty chapters.

One: probabilities stack. If you can score the next word given everything before it, you can score a whole paragraph. Multiply the chance of the first word by the chance of the second given the first, and so on. This isn’t a trick or an approximation. It’s an identity from probability theory called the chain rule, and it’s exact.

Equation 1.1 · A whole passage, broken into one-word guesses
P(w1,…,wT) = ∏t=1T P(wt∣w1:t−1)
In words: the probability of a whole passage is what you get by walking through it word by word, asking “how much did the model bet on the word that actually showed up here?”, and multiplying all those bets together. Which means: solve one word, and you’ve solved everything. Tap any part to unpack it.

Two: that product is a horrible number. Multiply a few hundred numbers smaller than 1 and you get something your laptop rounds to zero. Take logarithms and the multiplying turns into adding, tiny numbers turn into manageable ones, and — bonus — the sum ends up meaning something on its own. We’ll get there in §1.4.

§1.2 The fix (attempt one)

Count, then divide

We need the odds of the next word given what came before. We have a pile of text where people already made those choices. So: go look.

To get the odds of oil after “filled the lamp with”, count how often that phrase shows up in the , count how often oil came next, divide. That’s it. That’s the model.

Equation 1.2 · Odds by counting
P^(w∣c) = count(c,w) count(c)
In words: out of all the times context c happened, what fraction of the time did word w come next? The little hat on the P is a reminder that this is a guess from a finite pile of text, not the truth.

There’s a problem, and it shows up immediately. If c means “everything before this point”, then basically every context in existence has happened zero times, and you’re dividing by zero.

So we cheat. Let the context be just the last couple of words. Look back one word and you’ve got a bigram model. Look back two, a trigram. Generally, look back n−1 words and you’ve got an .

A note on vocabulary before we go further. I’ve been saying word, and for this chapter’s counting model that’s literally accurate — it counts words. Everywhere else in this book the unit is a , which is not a word, and the difference turns out to set your bill and your context limit. That’s Chapter 2’s whole job. Until then, read the two as roughly the same and enjoy it while it lasts.

Back to the cheat. It’s a claim about language: that words far away stop mattering. Which is obviously false. We do it anyway, because it makes counting possible. Get comfortable with that move now, while it’s cheap — a wrong-but-useful assumption doing real work until it breaks is the standard rhythm of this entire field.

Try it Pick a context, then turn n up. Watch two numbers move in opposite directions: how confident the guess is, and how much evidence it’s built on.

Notice: longer contexts give sharper, more specific guesses — when they’re found at all, which gets rarer fast. At n=5 most contexts appeared exactly once, so “certainty” means one person made one choice one time. Confidence and evidence have come apart.

That last sentence deserves a name, because it comes back at every scale of this subject. A model can be confident for two completely different reasons: because the evidence is overwhelming, or because the evidence is a single example. Nothing in the number tells you which. Chapter 12 builds the measurement discipline that keeps those apart. Chapter 27 treats mixing them up as a reliability failure.

§1.3 So what

Anything that can guess can also write

Nothing about that model was designed to produce text. It only scores options. But you can run a scorer forward: draw a word from the odds, stick it on the end, ask again. Draw, append, ask. Forever.

That loop is how every system in this book writes, including the ones we won’t reach for twenty-five chapters. The loop is the easy part. The odds are the hard part.

Which means we can just look at what our counting model has to say. It’ll diagnose itself better than I can describe it.

Exhibit 1.4 · interactive
Writing, with the copying made visible

Try it Generate at n=2. Then at n=5. Highlighted stretches are six or more words lifted word-for-word out of the corpus.

Notice: at low n it’s grammatical for three words at a time and forgets what it’s talking about instantly. At high n it reads beautifully and is almost entirely highlighted, because it’s reciting. There’s no setting that’s both original and coherent. That gap is the finding.

Both failures come from the same place, which is nice, because it means there’s one thing to fix.

With a short context, the model has tons of evidence and no idea what’s going on. With a long context, it knows exactly what’s going on and has one example to go by, so all it can do is replay it. The dial isn’t trading quality against cost. It’s trading coherence against plagiarism, and both ends are useless.

A quick word about temperature

The generator has a second knob, temperature, which flattens or sharpens the odds before drawing. Turn it down and the model always takes its favourite. Turn it up and long shots get a real chance. Worth playing with, because it comes back in Chapter 11 as an actual product decision.

But notice what it can’t do: no temperature setting adds knowledge that isn’t in the counts. A knob on how you sample is not a knob on what the model knows. People confuse those two constantly.

§1.4 So what

Surprise, per token

“It reads badly” isn’t a measurement. If the job is guessing, the obvious test is: show the model text it’s never seen, and ask how much money it put on what actually happened.

Big bets on the truth, good. Small bets on the truth, bad. By convention we work in logarithms — that turns Equation 1.1’s multiplying into adding — and flip the sign so that lower is better.

In base 2, the number you get is bits of surprise. It answers: how many yes/no questions’ worth of uncertainty did the model still have here? Zero bits means it knew. One bit means it was down to a coin flip. Ten bits means it was as lost as someone guessing between a thousand options.

Equation 1.3 · Average surprise per token — the score that trains everything
L= −1T ∑t=1T log2 P(wt∣w1:t−1)
In words: at every position, take the bet the model placed on the token that actually appeared, turn it into bits of surprise, and average. That’s — the same number that will train every model in this book. Here we’re just using it to grade one.

Averaging per token is what makes the number comparable across texts of different lengths, which the raw product wasn’t. And if bits don’t feel intuitive, exponentiate:

Equation 1.4 · Perplexity — surprise, restated as “how many options”
PPL= 2 L
In words: turns bits back into a count of options. Loss of 4 bits means perplexity 16: on average the model was about as unsure as someone picking blindly from 16 options. Lower is better, 1 would be psychic.

Notice: with no smoothing the loss is infinite, and the exhibit counts the words responsible. Each one is the model announcing that something which then happened was impossible. Any smoothing at all kills the infinity and reveals the model’s real, thoroughly mediocre quality underneath.

Here’s the thing about zero probability: it isn’t caution. It’s certainty. A model that never saw “grandson” after “the baker’s” and therefore rules it out has confused I didn’t see it with it can’t happen.

The standard patch is to hold back a little probability for everything. Add a small number k to every count before dividing, as if you’d seen each word a fraction of a time.

Equation 1.5 · Add-k smoothing — buying insurance
P^k(w∣c) = count(c,w)+k count(c)+kV
In words: pretend you saw every word k extra times after this context. The bottom grows by kV so the odds still add to 1 — meaning the insurance gets paid for out of the words you actually did see.

Smoothing turns an infinite loss into a finite one. But look at what it doesn’t do.

The probability it hands the unseen word is the same probability it hands every other unseen word in the vocabulary. After “the baker’s”, our model rates grandson and Tuesday and xylophone as exactly equally likely. It has no way to say that one of those is a bit like something it’s seen before.

Hang onto that sentence. It’s the hinge the next three chapters swing on.

§1.5 Where it breaks

You can’t fix this with more data

The obvious objection to everything above: the corpus is tiny, use more text. Let’s take that seriously, because the answer decides whether this chapter was a warm-up or a dead end.

Then the growth view shows the consequence: the vocabulary keeps climbing as the corpus grows and shows no sign of stopping. Rare things aren’t a finite list you can eventually finish collecting.

So the objection fails, but it fails in a useful way.

More data does help. Coverage really improves, and this is a big part of why scale matters so much later. What more data can’t do is change what kind of thing the model is.

A counting model treats every context as an atom with no relatives. It has seen “filled the lamp with oil” and it has learned precisely nothing about “filled the lantern with oil”. Every context needs its own evidence, and there will always be more contexts than evidence. Always. That’s not a corpus problem, it’s an architecture problem.

Now say that as a requirement, and the next three chapters basically write themselves:

Similar contexts need to be able to share evidence. For that, the model needs some way of representing words where “similar” is even a thing you can say — which a token’s ID number very much is not. That’s Chapter 2 (what are the units?), Chapter 3 (what does the model turn them into?), and Chapter 4 (what structure does that pick up, and from what?).

Your turn — write it before you read mine

In four or five sentences, tell a colleague why counting words fails as a way to model language. No using the words sparsity or generalise.

One way to say it. A counting model answers “what’s next?” by looking for the exact same run of preceding words somewhere in its pile of text and reporting what followed. To make a good guess it needs a long run of preceding words — but the longer the run, the less often it appears in the pile at all. Usually once, or never. When it appears once, the model reports that single continuation with total confidence. When it appears never, the model has nothing to say, and treats every word in the language as equally plausible. It can’t use the fact that a nearly identical run of words was in the pile, because it has no way to say that two runs of words are nearly identical. Every context is a lookup key that either matches or doesn’t.

Check yourself: did you get the overconfidence-from-one-example part, as well as the nothing-to-say part? Both come from the same lookup-key design, and the second one is the one people remember.

§1.6 How sure are we

What you’re allowed to say about a good guesser

We’ve defined a task — hand out odds over what comes next — and built one bad machine that does it. Before moving on, let’s be careful about what a system trained on this task is, because the words people use around it will otherwise wreck your reasoning for the next thirty chapters.

Three claims. They are not worth the same.

Established A trained language model computes odds over the next token, and that computation can be inspected, measured, and reproduced. Its surprise on unseen text is a real number that goes down as the modelling gets better. Everything technical in this book lives at this level.

Inferred A system that guesses extremely well must have internal machinery that tracks things like syntax, entities, topic, and tone — because you can’t hit that accuracy otherwise. This is a strong, well-supported inference, and Chapter 28 goes looking for that machinery directly. But it stays an inference. “It must be representing something like X” is a hypothesis about the inside, drawn from the outside.

Open Whether the thing understands what it’s reading. The training objective doesn’t answer this. Neither does a benchmark. It’s open in a specific sense: thoughtful people disagree about what would even count as evidence. Chapter 32 comes back to it deliberately, at the end, when you’ve got enough machinery to argue about it usefully.

The practical habit is to notice which of the three levels a claim is on — especially when the person making it is selling you something. “Our model understands your documents” is an Open claim wearing the grammar of an Established one. The right response isn’t cynicism about the product. It’s asking for the measurement.

Transfer — a real decision, not a definition

A team proposes measuring their assistant’s quality by its perplexity on a sample of your company’s support tickets, comparing two candidate models. What’s the first thing you ask?

CorrectPerplexity is an average per token. Two models that chop text into different units are reporting averages with different denominators — not comparable, no matter how carefully you measure. And a model that already trained on those tickets will look magnificent and tell you nothing. Chapter 2 makes the denominator concrete, Chapter 12 makes contamination a discipline. C and B are good questions too. They just aren’t the ones that decide whether the comparison means anything at all.
Have another lookIt’s D. Your pick is a fair question about validity, but it assumes the two numbers can be compared in the first place. Perplexity is per token, and how many tokens a text becomes is a property of the model’s tokenizer, not of the text. Different denominators, incomparable averages. Contamination is the second killer: a model trained on those tickets wins the bake-off and loses in production.
What you’re carrying into Chapter 2
  • Settled: the job is odds over the next token. The chain rule means solving one step solves everything. Average surprise per token measures it, and perplexity is that restated as a number of options.
  • Broken: counting can’t share evidence between similar contexts, so it’s either incoherent or reciting, and no amount of data changes that.
  • Still open, and load-bearing: I’ve been saying “token” like it’s obvious. It isn’t. Before we can represent a token we have to decide what one is — and that decision turns out to set your bill, your context limit, and whether two models’ scores can be compared at all.
