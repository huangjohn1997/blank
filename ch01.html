The guessing game

There’s one task underneath all of this. Once you see what it does and doesn’t ask for, half the weirdness of these systems stops being weird.

§1.1 The problem

You already do this, constantly

Read this and stop at the blank. “He filled the lamp with ___.”

Something showed up before you went looking for it. Probably oil. Maybe kerosene. Almost certainly not Tuesday — and notice, Tuesday isn’t ungrammatical there. It fits the slot fine. It’s just that nothing you know about lamps ever put it in the running.

Now this one. “The keeper ___.”

Nothing shows up. Or rather, a dozen things show up and not one of them insists. The blank is genuinely open.

Those two blanks are the whole chapter, and here’s the catch that falls out of them. A machine that continues text has to handle both cases with the same equipment, which means it can’t just name its favourite word. In the second case, naming a favourite would be a lie — it would hide the fact that the sentence hasn’t decided yet.

So whatever the machine hands back has to carry two things at once. Which words are plausible, and how much the context has narrowed things down.

That “spread of odds” has a name — a — and producing one is the entire job description of a . Not answering. Not understanding. Handing out confidence across a fixed list of options.

The “adds up to 1” part does more work than it looks like. It means the machine can’t be enthusiastic about everything. Belief is a fixed budget, so spending more on oil means spending less on everything else. That’s what makes the output informative rather than just cheerful, and it’s what lets us grade it in §1.4.

Pick one before reading on

Someone proposes simplifying all this: have the model output just its single best word instead of a whole spread of odds. What do you lose?

CorrectAnd that lost information is worth real money later. Knowing when a model is unsure is what lets a product hedge, ask a clarifying question, or hand off to a human instead of confidently inventing something. Chapter 27 is entirely about this. B is also true, and much less important.
Not quiteIt’s C. Both blanks in §1.1 have a best guess. Only one of them has a best guess worth trusting, and the spread is where that difference lives. Throw it away and you can’t tell a confident answer from a shrug — which is the machinery behind most embarrassing incidents in this field.
§1.2 The fix (attempt one)

Look it up

We want odds on the next word. We have a pile of text where people already made those choices. So the obvious move: go and look.

To get the odds on oil after “filled the lamp with”, find every place that phrase appears in the , see what came next each time, and report the proportions. That’s the whole model. For decades, it was the model.

Concretely, in our little corpus: “the keeper” shows up 12 times, and twice it’s followed by had. So the odds on had are 2 out of 12, about 17%. Do that for every continuation and you have your spread.

One decision is left, and it turns out to be the whole ballgame: how much of the preceding text do you match on? Match the last word only and you’ll find plenty of examples. Match the last twenty and you’ll find none. Looking back one word is called a bigram model, two words a trigram, and in general looking back n−1 words gives you an model. That n is the only dial on the thing.

A note on vocabulary before we go on. I keep saying word, and for this chapter’s model that’s literally accurate — it counts words. Everywhere else in this book the unit is a , which is not a word, and the difference ends up setting your bill and your context limit. That’s Chapter 2’s entire job. Until then, treat the two as the same thing and enjoy it while it lasts.

Try it Pick a context, then turn n up. Two numbers move in opposite directions: how sharp the guess is, and how much evidence sits behind it.

Notice: longer contexts give sharper, more specific guesses — when they’re found at all, which gets rarer fast. At n=5 most contexts appeared exactly once, so “certainty” means one person made one choice one time. Confidence and evidence have come apart.

That deserves a name, because it comes back at every scale of this subject. A model can be confident for two completely different reasons: the evidence is overwhelming, or the evidence is a single example. Nothing in the number tells you which one you’re looking at. Chapter 12 builds the discipline that keeps them apart. Chapter 27 treats confusing them as a reliability failure.

§1.3 So what

Anything that can guess can also write

Nothing about that lookup model was built to produce text. It only scores options. But look again at Figure 1.1: if you can score options, you can draw one, stick it on the end, and ask again. Draw, append, ask.

That loop is how every system in this book writes, including the ones we won’t reach for twenty-five chapters. Worth sitting with for a second, because it means the difference between a toy and a frontier model isn’t the writing part. It’s the quality of the spread.

So let’s look at what our lookup model has to say. It diagnoses itself better than I could describe it.

Exhibit 1.4 · interactive
Writing, with the copying made visible

Try it Generate at n=2. Then at n=5. Highlighted stretches are six or more words lifted word-for-word out of the corpus.

Notice: at low n it’s grammatical for three words at a time and forgets what it’s talking about instantly. At high n it reads beautifully and is almost entirely highlighted, because it’s reciting. No setting is both original and coherent. That gap is the finding.

Both failures come from the same place, which is good news, because it means there’s one thing to fix.

With a short context, the model has plenty of evidence and no idea what’s going on. With a long context, it knows exactly what’s going on and has one example to work from, so all it can do is replay it. The dial isn’t trading quality against cost. It’s trading coherence against plagiarism, and both ends are useless.

A quick word about temperature

The generator has a second knob, temperature, which flattens or sharpens the odds before drawing. Turn it down and the model always takes its favourite. Turn it up and long shots get a real chance. Worth playing with, because it comes back in Chapter 11 as an actual product decision.

But notice what it can’t do: no temperature setting adds knowledge that isn’t already in the spread. A knob on how you sample is not a knob on what the model knows. People mix those up constantly, usually while explaining why a model made something up.

§1.4 So what

Putting a number on “badly”

“It reads badly” isn’t a measurement. If the job is guessing, the obvious test is: show the model text it has never seen, and ask how much it bet on what actually happened.

Big bets on the truth, good. Small bets on the truth, bad.

That’s the whole idea, and it is the single most important number in this book. It’s how every model in every later chapter gets trained, and it’s how they all get compared. It gets reported as surprise, in bits, averaged over the text — and bits are worth a minute of your time, because they’re the currency of everything downstream.

Get a feel for this and you can skip a lot of algebra

Surprise is just “how big a bet did you place on the thing that actually happened”, flipped so lower is better.

Notice: with no safety net, the loss is infinite, and the exhibit counts the words responsible. Each one is the model announcing that something which then happened was impossible. A single one poisons the entire score — so the number can’t tell a nearly-good model from a hopeless one.

Zero probability isn’t caution. It’s certainty. A model that never saw “grandson” after “the baker’s”, and therefore rules it out, has confused I didn’t see it with it can’t happen. That confusion is one of the recurring villains of this book.

The standard patch is to hold back a little probability for everything, so nothing is ever flatly impossible. Pretend you saw every word a fraction of a time after every context. That’s exactly what the smoothing slider does.

And it works. The infinity goes away and you get a finite, honest, thoroughly mediocre score. But look at how it works, because this is where the chapter turns.

The probability smoothing hands to the unseen word is the same probability it hands every other unseen word in the vocabulary. After “the baker’s”, our model rates grandson and Tuesday and xylophone as exactly equally likely. It has no way to say that one of them is a bit like something it has seen before.

Hang onto that sentence. It’s the hinge the next three chapters swing on.

§1.5 Where it breaks

You can’t fix this with more data

The obvious objection to all of the above: my corpus is tiny, use more text. Let’s take it seriously, because the answer decides whether this chapter was a warm-up or a dead end.

So the objection fails, but it fails in a useful way.

More data does help. Coverage really improves, and that’s a big part of why scale matters so much later in this book. What more data can’t do is change what kind of thing the model is.

A lookup model treats every context as an atom with no relatives. It has seen “filled the lamp with oil” and it has learned precisely nothing about “filled the lantern with oil”. Every context needs its own evidence, and there will always be more contexts than evidence. Always. That’s not a data problem, it’s a design problem.

Now state it as a requirement, and the next three chapters basically write themselves:

Similar things need to be able to share evidence. Which means the model needs a way of representing words where “similar” is even a thing you can say — and a word’s identity, as a label in a list, flatly isn’t. That’s Chapter 2 (what are the units?), Chapter 3 (what does the model turn them into?), and Chapter 4 (what structure does that pick up, and where does it come from?).

Your turn — write it before you read mine

In four or five sentences, tell a colleague why looking words up in a big pile of text fails as a way to model language. Don’t use the words sparsity or generalise.

One way to say it. A lookup model answers “what’s next?” by finding the same run of preceding words somewhere in its pile of text and reporting what followed. To make a good guess it needs a long run of preceding words — but the longer the run, the less often it appears in the pile at all. Usually once, or never. When it appears once, the model reports that single continuation with total confidence. When it appears never, the model has nothing to say, and treats every word in the language as equally plausible. And it can’t lean on the fact that a nearly identical run of words was in the pile, because it has no way to say that two runs of words are nearly identical. Every context is a key that either matches or doesn’t.

Check yourself: did you get the overconfidence-from-one-example half, as well as the nothing-to-say half? Both come from the same design, and the second is the half people remember.

§1.6 How sure are we

What you’re allowed to say about a good guesser

We’ve defined a task — hand out odds on what comes next — and built one bad machine that does it. Before moving on, let’s be careful about what a system trained on this task actually is. Otherwise the words people use around it will wreck your reasoning for the next thirty chapters.

Three claims. They aren’t worth the same, and this book keeps them labelled.

Established A trained language model produces odds on what comes next, and that can be inspected, measured, and reproduced. Its surprise on unseen text is a real number that goes down as the modelling gets better. Everything technical in this book lives here.

Inferred A system that guesses extremely well must have internal machinery tracking things like grammar, entities, topic, and tone — because you can’t reach that accuracy otherwise. Strong, well-supported, and still an inference. “It must be representing something like X” is a claim about the inside, made from the outside. Chapter 28 goes looking directly.

Open Whether the thing understands what it reads. The training task doesn’t answer this. Neither does a benchmark score. It’s open in a specific sense: thoughtful people disagree about what would even count as evidence. Chapter 32 comes back to it on purpose, at the end, when you have enough machinery to argue about it usefully.

The practical habit is noticing which of the three a claim belongs to — especially when the person making it is selling you something. “Our model understands your documents” is an Open claim wearing the grammar of an Established one. The right response isn’t cynicism about the product. It’s asking for the measurement.

Transfer — a real decision, not a definition

A team proposes judging two candidate models by their average surprise on a sample of your company’s support tickets. What’s the first thing you ask?

CorrectThe score is an average per unit of text, and two models may not use the same units — so the averages have different denominators and can’t be compared, however carefully you measure. That’s Chapter 2. And a model that already trained on those tickets will look magnificent and tell you nothing; that’s Chapter 12. C and B are good questions too. They just aren’t the ones that decide whether the comparison means anything at all.
Have another lookIt’s D. Your pick is a fair question, but it assumes the two numbers can be compared in the first place. The score is an average per unit of text, and how many units a ticket becomes depends on the model — different denominators, incomparable averages. Then there’s contamination: a model that already trained on those tickets wins the bake-off and loses in production.
What you’re carrying into Chapter 2
  • Settled: the job is odds on what comes next. Solve one step and you’ve solved passages of any length. Average surprise on unseen text is how you measure it.
  • Broken: looking things up can’t share evidence between similar contexts, so it either babbles or recites — and no amount of data changes that.
  • Still open, and load-bearing: I’ve been saying “word” like it’s obvious. It isn’t. Before we can represent one, we have to decide what one is — and that decision sets your bill, your context limit, and whether two models’ scores can be compared at all.

If you go and read the papers — the three formulas behind this chapter

You don’t need these to continue, and this book won’t ask you to manipulate them. They’re here so the notation is recognisable when you meet it in a technical report. Tap any highlighted piece for a plain reading of it.

1 · Scoring a whole passage — the chain rule
P(w1,…,wT) = ∏t=1T P(wt∣w1:t−1)
In words: multiply together, across every position, the probability the model gave to the word that actually appeared there. The formal version of “solve one step and you’ve solved everything”.
2 · Average surprise, and perplexity
L= −1T ∑t=1T log2 P(wt∣…) PPL=2L
In words: the minus sign and the log₂ turn a probability into bits of surprise. The ∑ and the 1/T just mean “take the average”. That is the entire content of the bits table above.
3 · The smoothing slider
P^k(w∣c) = count(c,w)+k count(c)+kV
In words: add a little to every tally, and add enough to the bottom that the odds still sum to 1. The consequence worth keeping: that safety net gets paid for out of the words you actually observed.
