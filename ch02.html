The representation bargain

Chapter 1 said “token” fourteen times without defining it. The definition turns out to be a negotiation, and everyone who uses the model pays for its terms.

§2.1 The need

Before the units, there are bytes

A model’s input is not text. It is a sequence of integers, each one an index into a fixed list. Chapter 1 assumed that list existed and moved on. Now we have to make it, and the first thing to establish is what raw material we are making it out of.

Text on a computer is a sequence of bytes. To interpret those bytes as characters you need an encoding, and in practice that encoding is UTF-8: every Unicode character is stored as one to four bytes, with the common ASCII characters taking one and everything else taking more. This is not trivia. It means that before any modelling decision is made, the world’s writing systems already have unequal costs — an English letter is one byte, a Chinese character is usually three, and an emoji is four.

What to notice: one visible character can be several codepoints and several bytes; two strings that render identically can differ in memory. If the tokenizer treats those two as different, then a model trained on one spelling of café has learned nothing about the other. This is why real pipelines normalise text before doing anything else — and why normalisation is a modelling decision, not a cleanup step.

So the honest starting point is: we have bytes, and we need a finite vocabulary of units to predict over. Everything else in this chapter follows from choosing those units badly and then well.

§2.2 Two obvious answers, both wrong

The bargain

The first obvious choice is characters. The vocabulary is tiny — a few hundred symbols covers most of what you will ever see, and nothing is ever unrepresentable. The cost is length: every sentence becomes five times as many prediction steps, and each step carries almost no information. The model must spend its capacity learning to spell before it can learn anything about meaning, and — a point that will matter enormously from Chapter 7 onward — the compute needed to relate every position to every other position grows with the square of the sequence length.

The second obvious choice is words. Now sequences are short and each unit is meaningful. But the vocabulary is unbounded: new words arrive constantly, and any fixed word list will meet text it cannot represent, which it must map to a single “unknown” token — an information hole in the input. Worse, the word list wastes itself on morphology, storing run, runs, running, and runner as four unrelated entries that share nothing.

§2.3 The mechanism

Learning the units by merging

Here is the whole algorithm. Start with every word split into individual characters — so the vocabulary begins as the character set, and nothing is unrepresentable. Then count every adjacent pair of symbols across the corpus, find the most frequent pair, and glue it together permanently: that pair becomes a new single symbol, added to the vocabulary. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the size you want.

Equation 2.1 · The one decision a tokenizer trainer makes, repeated
(a,b) = arg max(x,y)∈pairs count(xy)
Read it as: among all adjacent symbol pairs currently in the corpus, take the one that occurs most often, and record it as the next merge. Applying that merge changes which pairs are adjacent, so the next round’s counts differ — the rule is simple, its consequences are not.

The method is called byte-pair encoding, borrowed from data compression and brought to machine translation by Sennrich, Haddow and Birch in 2016 to solve exactly the rare-word problem of §2.2. Established Its close relatives — WordPiece, which chooses merges by likelihood gain rather than raw frequency, and unigram language-model segmentation — differ in the objective, not in the shape of the idea.

Sennrich, Haddow & Birch, “Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units” (2016). Kudo & Richardson, SentencePiece (2018), for the whitespace-marker convention used in the exhibit below.

Two things are worth extracting from what you just watched.

First, the vocabulary is not a list of words someone chose; it is a trace of the training corpus’s statistics. A tokenizer trained mostly on English news will represent English news in few, large units and everything else in many, small ones. That inheritance is invisible in the model card and permanent in the product.

Second, the merge list is ordered, and applying it in order is what makes segmentation deterministic: the same string always produces the same tokens. That is what makes the next section possible.

§2.4 First consequence

Reversible, deterministic, and frozen

A trained tokenizer does two jobs. Encode: apply the learned merges to a string and emit token IDs. Decode: concatenate the tokens back into a string. Because the whitespace marker is part of the token text, decoding restores the spaces, and encode-then-decode returns what you started with. Lossless round-tripping matters more than it sounds: it means the model’s output can be turned back into text without a second guessing step.

Exhibit 2.4 · interactive · type your own text
Encoding, decoding, and where the seams fall

Do this Type a rare word, a number, a name, and a typo. Watch where the tokenizer breaks them, and confirm the round-trip stays exact.

What to notice: common words survive whole; rare words shatter into fragments that carry no relation to their meaning; digits fragment by frequency rather than by place value. The round-trip is exact, but the units are wildly uneven in how much meaning each carries — and the model sees only the units.

Now the consequence that experienced practitioners treat as a hard constraint: the tokenizer is frozen before pretraining begins. Every weight in the model is learned against these specific units. Changing the vocabulary afterwards invalidates the input and output layers — the model would have to relearn what its integers mean. So the tokenizer is not preprocessing. It is part of the architecture, decided earliest, with the least information, and revisable only at the cost of retraining. Inferred from the mechanism, and consistent with what disclosed technical reports describe when they document tokenizer choices alongside architecture rather than alongside data cleaning.

§2.5 Second consequence

Who pays for the units

Because merges are awarded by frequency, whatever was frequent in the tokenizer’s training text gets efficient representation, and whatever was rare gets shredded. Now recall that almost every commercial quantity in this field is measured per token: the price of an API call, the size of the context window, the latency of generation, the throughput of a server. Put those two facts together.

The measure is fertility: how many tokens a tokenizer spends per word (or per character) of a given text. Higher fertility means the same meaning costs more.

Equation 2.2 · Fertility — tokens spent per unit of meaning
F= |tokens(s)| |words(s)|
Read it as: count the tokens a tokenizer produces for a string, divide by the number of words in it. F = 1 means one token per word. F = 3 means every word costs three prediction steps, three slots of context window, and three units of billing.

What to notice: our tokenizer saw only English, so non-English text falls back to near-character segmentation and its fertility multiplies. This is an exaggerated version of a real and documented effect: production tokenizers are trained on many languages and the gap narrows, but it does not close, because merges remain a finite budget allocated by frequency. The consequence is that identical products cost different amounts to serve in different languages — and the cheaper language is usually the one that dominated the tokenizer’s training data.

What to notice: per-token prices are not comparable across models with different tokenizers, and neither are per-token quality metrics. The fix is mechanical: convert both to a shared denominator — cost per thousand words of your actual traffic — before comparing anything.

Equation 2.3 · Converting a per-token price into a comparable one
C1k words = p· F· 1000
Read it as: price per token, times tokens per word, times a thousand words. Two models with different tokenizers can only be compared after this conversion — and F must be measured on your text, not on English news.
§2.6 The limit

What the model can and cannot see

We now have a finite vocabulary, a deterministic encoder, and a reversible decoder. We also have a set of failures that follow directly from the units, and that you will recognise in products.

The model cannot see inside a token. If strawberry is three tokens, the letter r is not an object the model has access to; counting its occurrences requires reconstructing spelling from statistics about token co-occurrence. Character-level tasks are therefore hard for reasons that have nothing to do with intelligence and everything to do with representation.

Numbers are segmented by frequency, not by magnitude. Whether 1997 is one token or three depends on how often that string appeared in the tokenizer’s corpus. Arithmetic over units that do not align with place value is doing work that its representation actively resists.

Per-token metrics are tokenizer-relative. Perplexity, cost, context length, and tokens-per-second all share this problem. Whenever you compare two systems on a per-token quantity, ask whose denominator you are using. Established

And now the limit that motivates the next chapter. We have turned text into a sequence of integers — token IDs. Ask what the model can do with the integer 4,412 as such. Is it near 4,411? Is it bigger than 87? The numbers are addresses, assigned in the arbitrary order the merges happened to be discovered, and any arithmetic on them is meaningless. Yet Chapter 1 established what we actually need: a representation in which similar things can share evidence. Token IDs are the opposite of that. They are maximally distinct, equally distant, and mutually uninformative.

Reconstruct — write it before you read it

A vendor offers you two models at the same quality. Model A costs $3 per million tokens; model B costs $2. Your traffic is customer support in English, Turkish, and Japanese. In four or five sentences, say what you would measure before choosing, and why the per-token price is not the answer.

A model answer. I would take a representative sample of real tickets in all three languages and run each through both tokenizers to measure fertility — tokens per word — per language. Cost per ticket is price per token times tokens per ticket, so a model that is 33% cheaper per token but 60% more fertile on Turkish is more expensive for that traffic. The same measurement decides two other things I care about: how much of each model’s context window a long ticket consumes, and how many generation steps a reply takes, which is latency. I would also refuse to compare any per-token quality number between the two until it was converted to a shared per-word or per-document denominator.

Check yourself: did your answer treat fertility as something to measure on your own traffic rather than to look up? And did you notice that the same measurement governs cost, context, and latency simultaneously — three product properties from one representation choice?

Transfer

A team wants to improve their model’s arithmetic by retraining the tokenizer so that every digit is its own token, keeping everything else identical. What is the honest summary of this proposal?

Committed · correctBoth halves matter. Digit-splitting genuinely aligns the units with place value — a representation fix for a representation failure — and it is expensive, because the tokenizer sits at the bottom of the architecture. Note also that D is backwards: per-digit tokens make numeric text longer, which is a real cost accepted in exchange for alignment.
Committed · not quiteThe answer is A. The idea is sound — it is one of the reasons some model families do exactly this — but the tokenizer is frozen before pretraining, so changing it invalidates every learned weight that touches the vocabulary. And it makes numeric text longer, not shorter.
Carry forward
  • Settled: units are learned from corpus statistics by repeated merging; segmentation is deterministic and reversible; the tokenizer is frozen before pretraining and therefore architectural.
  • Consequences to keep: fertility differs by language and domain, so per-token prices, context windows, latencies, and perplexities are all tokenizer-relative; convert to a shared denominator before comparing.
  • Unresolved, and load-bearing: a token ID is an arbitrary address. It supports no notion of similarity, so it cannot let related contexts share evidence — the exact thing Chapter 1 proved we need.
