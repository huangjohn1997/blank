Chapter One

The Guessing Game

Prediction, compression, and the first machine that did it by counting.

Draft A · senior-engineer voice—

1.1An experiment from 1951

Here is a sentence with the last word removed.

The engineer torqued the bolt until it

You already have a guess. Probably a short list — stopped, snapped, seated, gave, broke. You did not consider pineapple. You did not consider the. Nobody explained the rules of that exercise to you, and you were not trying. You have been running it one word ahead of every sentence you have ever heard.

In 1951 Claude Shannon turned that reflex into a measurement. He sat a person down with a passage of English they had not read, covered it, and asked them to guess the first letter. Wrong, he told them so and they guessed again. Right, he moved to the next letter. What he wrote down was not the text. It was the number of guesses per letter.

Before we go further, play it. Guess the next character in the passage below. There is no penalty and no timer, and the machine will keep score in the way Shannon kept score.

That number is the whole chapter. A reader who needs one guess most of the time is reading text that barely tells them anything new — the characters were already determined by what came before. A reader who needs eleven guesses is being genuinely informed. Shannon had shown three years earlier how to convert those guess counts into a quantity in bits, and when he ran the arithmetic on English prose he got something close to one bit per character.

Shannon was measuring his subjects. But he had also, without setting out to, defined the target that every system in this book is aiming at, and set the score to beat.

1.2Why prediction is not a parlor trick

Go back to the bolt. Ask what you had to have in your head to produce that short list of candidates.

You knew that torqued is something done to fasteners. You knew that a bolt is a fastener, and that fasteners fail by shearing or stripping rather than by melting. You knew that until demands a change of state, so the blank wants a verb and not an adjective. You knew that it refers to the bolt and not to the engineer, which is a genuinely hard inference that you made without noticing. You knew English word order well enough that the was never a candidate.

None of that is vocabulary. All of it is required to score well on a task that looks like a party game.

Here is the claim the rest of this book rests on: a system that predicts the next word well enough has to represent the things that determine the next word. There is no cheap route. You cannot fake your way to a low error rate on English text without picking up syntax, and then facts, and then the way objects behave, because those are the things that decide which word comes next.

Where this breaks

That claim is an argument, not a theorem, and it has a real limit. Prediction pressure only forces a system to represent what actually leaves a trace in the statistics. If something is true about bolts but never written down, no amount of next-word training will recover it. And a system can score well on a pattern for the wrong reason — matching the shape of an answer rather than the substance. Both failures come back in Chapter 12, and neither is hypothetical.

1.3Prediction and compression are the same problem

In 1948 Shannon published the paper that created information theory, and buried in it is a fact worth carrying for the next four hundred pages: you do not have to transmit what the receiver can already predict.

Say you are sending English one character at a time and both ends agree on a predictor. Give short codes to the characters your predictor thinks are likely and long codes to the ones it thinks are unlikely. Common cases cost few bits. Surprises cost many. The better the predictor, the shorter the message — and the improvement is not incidental, it is exactly proportional.

Scroll the next figure. Each character's width is the number of bits it costs to send.

Step 1 — no model

With no model of English, every character is equally surprising and costs the same 4.75 bits. This is the honest price of ignorance, and it is what the sentence costs if you send it as raw symbols.

Step 2 — letter frequencies

Now the sender knows only how often each letter appears in English. E and T and the space get short codes, Q and Z get long ones. Nothing about context yet — and the message is already shorter.

Step 3 — one character of context

The model now knows which characters tend to follow which. After q it is nearly certain of u, so u becomes almost free. Watch the common sequences collapse to slivers while the word-initial characters stay wide — those are the ones still carrying the information.

What this buys you

Compression is therefore a scoreboard. If your model of English encodes a book in fewer bits than mine, your model is better than mine, and nobody had to define "better" first or convene a panel to judge it. That is unusual, and it is why this field could make progress: it had a number.

It also means you should be careful with the deflationary version of what these systems do. "It only predicts the next word" is true. It is not small.

1.4The first machine that did it by counting

The oldest working language model predates computers and was built by someone with no interest in language.

In 1913 Andrei Markov worked through twenty thousand letters of Pushkin's Eugene Onegin, sorting each into vowel or consonant, and counted how often each followed the other. He was in an argument about probability theory — a colleague had claimed that the law of large numbers required independent events, and Markov wanted a concrete case of dependent events where it held anyway. Pushkin was a convenient corpus. He got his result, and he left behind the tool.

The tool is this. To assign a probability to a whole sentence, walk through it word by word, and at each step ask how likely that word was given the few words before it. Then multiply.

P(w1, w2, … , wT)  =  ∏t  P(wt | wt−1, wt−2) In: a sentence of T words. Out: one number between 0 and 1. The window here is two words back, which makes this a trigram model. You will meet this product again as the training objective of every model in this book.

A model that looks back n−1 words is an n-gram model: bigram for one word back, trigram for two, and so on. Fitting one requires no mathematics beyond division. Take a pile of text, count how many times torqued the bolt appears, count how many times torqued the appears, and divide. That ratio is your probability. You now have a language model, and you can build one on paper.

You can also run it forwards to generate text, which is where it stops being abstract. Shannon did this himself in the 1948 paper, by hand, with a book and a table of word frequencies:

1.5The wall

Take a vocabulary of fifty thousand words, which is modest. A trigram model needs a probability for every ordered triple. Scroll, and count the cells.

n = 2

A bigram model over fifty thousand words needs 2.5 billion cells. Large, but a big corpus genuinely covers the ones that matter, and this is why bigram models worked at all.

n = 3

One more word of context multiplies by fifty thousand: 1.25 × 1014 cells. You have just crossed the corpus line. From here on, most of your table is empty and always will be.

n = 4, 5, 6

Each step multiplies by fifty thousand again. By six words of context you need 1.6 × 1028 cells — more cells than there are atoms in a person. The table grows exponentially in the window and your corpus does not.

Not a data problem

This is the part that is easy to get wrong. Zipf's law tells you word frequencies fall off brutally, so the triples you are missing are not a random sample you could fix by scraping harder. They are missing structurally. You cannot out-collect exponential growth.

Zipf is worth seeing directly, because it is the reason the gaps are not random. Here is the actual word-frequency distribution of the corpus the model above was fitted on.

Statisticians spent two decades on the sparsity problem and produced genuinely clever machinery for it: back off to shorter windows when a long one has no data, shave probability mass off what you did observe and redistribute it to what you did not. It works, in the sense that the numbers improve. It does not touch the underlying failure, which is this:

Your model has counted torqued the bolt nine hundred times, and it has never once seen torqued the screw. It therefore assigns screw a probability of zero, or whatever floor the smoothing gives it. And there is nothing in the model — not one number anywhere in that table — that records the fact that a bolt and a screw are almost the same object.

What the model has

To a counting model, bolt and screw and Wednesday are three unrelated symbols that happen to be spelled differently. Every word is an island. Evidence gathered about one is worth precisely nothing to any other.

The name for it

Yoshua Bengio put the name on it in 2003, and it is the name we will use: the curse of dimensionality. Discrete symbols in a large vocabulary can never share statistical evidence, so a counting model has to see every combination it will ever be asked about.

What we need instead

Put the words somewhere. Give each one a position such that similar words land near each other, and nine hundred observations of bolt start teaching the model about screw for free. Sparsity stops being a wall and becomes an approximation problem — which is a kind of problem engineers know how to attack.

Real constraint or inherited?

Sort what this chapter established. Real: language is statistically predictable and there is a measurable ceiling on how predictable; predicting well requires representing structure; prediction and compression are one problem. Those are not going to change.

Inherited: that the mechanism is counting. That words are indivisible symbols. That the window is a fixed number of words. Every one of those is a design choice, all three get discarded in the next four chapters, and none of them were ever laws. Keep the two columns separate in your head all semester — you will be asked which column a mechanism belongs in every week.

1.6What breaks next

Shannon gave us a target and a scoreboard. Markov gave us a machine. The machine hits a wall that is combinatorial, which means no amount of engineering effort on the machine itself will get past it.

The representation we need is the subject of Chapter 2. The idea for it was published in 1986, the version that worked arrived in 2003, and it took until 2013 for anyone to make it cheap enough to matter — which tells you something about this field that no architecture diagram will. Most of the ideas were already lying around. What changed was what you could afford to run.

Chapter One

Introduction to Statistical Language Modeling

Draft B · conventional register—

1.1Overview

In this chapter, we introduce the foundational concepts of statistical language modeling. We begin with a review of information-theoretic preliminaries, proceed to a formal definition of the language modeling objective, and conclude with an analysis of n-gram models and their limitations. Familiarity with basic probability is assumed.

1.2Information-theoretic preliminaries

The information content of a discrete random variable is quantified by its entropy. For a random variable X with probability mass function p(x), the entropy is defined as:

H(X) = −∑x p(x) log2 p(x)

Entropy is measured in bits and may be interpreted as the expected number of bits required to encode a realization of X under an optimal code. It is important to note that entropy is maximized for the uniform distribution and minimized for a deterministic one.

Shannon (1948) established the fundamental relationship between the entropy of a source and the minimum achievable expected code length, a result known as the source coding theorem. Shannon (1951) subsequently estimated the entropy rate of printed English using a human-subject prediction protocol, obtaining estimates in the range of approximately 0.6 to 1.3 bits per character. This may be contrasted with log2(27) ≈ 4.75 bits per character under a uniform distribution over the 26 letters and the space character, indicating substantial redundancy in natural language.

The relationship between prediction and compression is a central theme of the literature and provides a principled evaluation criterion: a model which assigns higher likelihood to held-out text induces a shorter encoding of that text, and is therefore considered superior. This motivates the use of perplexity as an evaluation metric, which will be introduced in Chapter 3.

1.3The language modeling objective

Let V denote a finite vocabulary and let w1, …, wT ∈ V denote a sequence of tokens. The objective of language modeling is to learn the joint distribution over such sequences. By the chain rule of probability, this joint distribution may be factorized autoregressively:

P(w1, …, wT) = ∏t=1T P(wt | w1, …, wt−1)

This factorization is exact and requires no independence assumptions. However, direct estimation of the conditional distributions is intractable, as the conditioning context grows without bound. It is therefore standard to introduce a Markov assumption of order n−1, under which the conditional distribution depends only on the preceding n−1 tokens. Markov (1913) provides an early application of such dependence structures to letter sequences in Russian literature.

1.4N-gram models

Under the Markov assumption, the resulting model is referred to as an n-gram model. The maximum likelihood estimate of each conditional probability is obtained by relative frequency estimation over a training corpus:

P̂(wt | wt−n+1, …, wt−1) = C(wt−n+1, …, wt) / C(wt−n+1, …, wt−1)

where C(·) denotes the count of the specified sequence in the training corpus. N-gram models may be utilized both for scoring and for generation, the latter being performed by ancestral sampling from the estimated conditional distributions. Shannon (1948) presents illustrative samples from low-order word approximations to English, which exhibit local fluency in the absence of global coherence.

Empirically, model quality improves with increasing n up to a point, beyond which the model reproduces training sequences verbatim as a consequence of data sparsity. Figure 1.2 reports local plausibility and verbatim reproduction rates as a function of n.

1.5Limitations

N-gram models are subject to two principal limitations.

First, the parameter space grows exponentially in n. For a vocabulary of size |V| = 50,000, a trigram model requires the estimation of |V|3 ≈ 1.25 × 1014 parameters, as shown in Figure 1.3. Given that corpus sizes grow at most linearly with collection effort, the majority of these parameters cannot be estimated from observed data. Compounding this, the Zipfian distribution of word frequencies ensures that unobserved n-grams are not uniformly distributed across the parameter space. A substantial literature addresses this issue through smoothing and backoff techniques (Katz, 1987; Kneser & Ney, 1995).

Second, and more fundamentally, n-gram models employ a discrete symbolic representation in which distinct vocabulary items are treated as mutually orthogonal. Consequently, statistical evidence acquired with respect to one token cannot inform estimates involving a semantically related token. Bengio et al. (2003) characterize this limitation as a manifestation of the curse of dimensionality, and it constitutes the primary motivation for the distributed representations introduced in the following chapter.

1.6Summary

  • Entropy quantifies the information content of a source; English exhibits an entropy rate of approximately 1 bit per character.
  • Prediction and compression are formally equivalent, motivating likelihood-based evaluation.
  • The joint distribution over sequences factorizes autoregressively by the chain rule.
  • N-gram models apply a Markov assumption and estimate conditionals by relative frequency.
  • N-gram models suffer from exponential parameter growth and an inability to generalize across related vocabulary items.

In the next chapter, we will introduce distributed representations of words and demonstrate how they address the generalization limitations identified above.

·The cost, stated honestly

Draft A is roughly twice as long for identical content, and it carries four interactive figures that Draft B delivers as three static ones. That is the real trade: at that ratio a fourteen-chapter book gets long, and a reader who already knows the material will find Draft A slow and Draft B efficient.

The bet is that the second reader is not the one you are writing for. But it implies a structural decision — Draft A's density belongs in the chapters carrying new mechanism, and something closer to Draft B belongs in reference material, appendices, and notation summaries. Worth choosing deliberately rather than drifting.
